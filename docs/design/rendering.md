### 10.7 HDR renditions

Two browsers, two answers. **Chrome** renders HDR stills, on desktop and on Android 14+, from a PQ- or HLG-tagged image. **Firefox** honours no HDR image tagging at all: a flat 50% grey reads 128 whether it carries a PQ cICP chunk or nothing, through a PNG and through a natively decoded JXL alike (§10.5). Its **video** pipeline does composite HDR, on Windows only, by passing the frame through to the compositor and the monitor. The underlying reason is the same for images on every platform and for video on most of them: Gecko's compositor is still 32-bit SDR, and RGBA16F framebuffers (bug 1889288) gate all of it.

So an HDR rendition is **one** file, a PQ AVIF, and Firefox is handed that same file and rewraps it as a video in the page (§10.7.2) - rather than two, the second a one-frame AV1 in an MP4 encoded and stored per photo at import.

There were six for a while, and a page at `/hdr-check` to look at them on: the same photo also as a 4:2:0 AVIF baseline control, and each of the three with an SDR reference to compare against. It answered the question it was built for - HDR output cannot be observed from script, since anything read back through a canvas has already been tone-mapped, so the only way to know whether a file lights up a panel was to put it next to one that should not and look. Once that was settled the page was a diagnostic nothing in the product reached, and it kept a whole second encode path alive behind it: an SDR variant with its own transfer and gamut conversion, a 4:2:0 medium, six renditions per photo, and a directory tree of its own under the data path. It is gone, and this section describes what ships. HLG was dropped: everything that renders HDR renders PQ, and PQ is absolute where HLG is relative to the display's own range. Encoding client-side was ruled out, though not for the reason first recorded here: Firefox 153 does expose `VideoEncoder` on Windows, measured. What it will not do is hand that encoder HDR pixels - `VideoFrame` rejects every 10-bit format (`I420P10 is unsupported`) and 4:4:4 with it, leaving 8-bit `I420` and `NV12` as the only planar input an encode could start from.

**Decode.** This is the path that makes anything HDR. Not three settings asked of a decoder - Rec.2020 primaries, an identity gamma curve, no auto-brightening - but what the decode does by construction: `decode_rawler` subtracts black, applies the as-shot white balance, takes the camera matrix through to Rec.2020 primaries, and brightens nothing anywhere. The last part matters most: auto-brightening normalises exposure, which spends exactly the headroom above diffuse white that carries the HDR. The result is scene-referred, so a normally exposed frame's mean sits far below the sRGB render's; which is what the integration test asserts, since a decode that quietly stopped applying these would still produce a plausible-looking file.

**A sensor colour the camera matrix takes outside Rec.2020 is brought in without turning** (`prelude::in_gamut`, through `assemble.slang`'s `coloured`), and what that is worth is measurable on a photograph rather than only on the light-painted arc the function's own comment records. DSC03422's Mazda decodes with blue against zero across the whole wing mirror - the camera matrix's blue row is `[-0.001, -0.236, 1.237]` and the sensor sees that red at G/R 0.25 - where the camera's own rendering puts it at B/R 0.093. Clipping the negative channel there leaves the other two where the matrix put them, so every pixel past the edge lands on the same hue and the region goes flat wherever the gamut's edge crosses a gradient. Pulling the colour toward its own luma instead keeps the hue and gives up only saturation, and the mirror's green moves about 3% against red.

**And it is a knee, not a wall: the lowest channel eases into `GAMUT_FLOOR` of the colour's luma, C¹ at twice that, rather than stopping at zero.** The matched arm applies it once, last, on the colour a still is coded from (`colour.slang`'s `matched_nits`). Pulled to exactly zero, a colour the match puts *near* the floor is a channel with half its pixels clamped and the other half carrying photosite noise, and PQ is steepest at black: on DSC03422's red hood the match had green at a thousandth of red, 49% of its samples at code zero and the rest swinging to a 99th percentile of 12952 codes. Invisible as light, and exactly what 4:2:0 hands the bright channel (§10.1). Held at the floor instead, the same noise is a small linear quantity around a positive level - green's 99th percentile 1883, no zeros - and whatever the fit does past the hull, the picture degrades continuously rather than breaking.

What it does *not* do is put the colour back, and the distinction matters before anyone reads a render as fixed. Pulling to the edge lands the lowest channel exactly on zero, so the mirror's blue is still zero and the paint still sits on a flat wall; nothing downstream can lift it either, since `curve(0)` is 0 and the camera's blue there sits on the crushed bound where the fit's pairs gate it out. Reaching the camera's B/R would need the edge itself to be soft - a compression along the chroma direction rather than a pull onto it - which is a look change on every saturated colour in every photograph rather than a repair.

**All of this is in `native/rawshim`** - the grade, the colour fit and the encode (`tone.rs`, `hdr_fit.rs`, `hdr_args.rs`, `hdr.rs`). It was TypeScript, and the graded frame was written to ffmpeg's stdin from there: ~115MB at 24MP and ~366MB at 61MP crossing the FFI boundary to reach a consumer that was never on this side of it. One call now takes a path and a job and produces the file, without the samples ever leaving.

**The port was held to a pin rather than to judgement** (`test/fixtures/hdr_grade.pin.txt`). It could not be verified by "the tests still pass": the grade's failure modes look like ordinary pictures. So the TypeScript's graded samples were recorded first, for five cases, and the Rust was required to match. It did, **bit-identically on all five**, every one of ~29.6M `u16` samples.

Worth recording how nearly that pin was useless. Perturbing the BT.2390 knee changed nothing, because `eetf` returns early when the frame already fits the display: at 1000 nits the fixture never reaches the roll-off, so the pin covered none of the subtlest arithmetic in the grade. Two `peakNits=203` cases fixed it, and a 0.5 → 0.501 shift then fails. A pin nobody tries to break is a pin that proves nothing.

**Encode.** **libaom in all-intra mode**, 12-bit, 4:2:0 by default with a setting to raise it. There were two media here and they diverged in container and in whether their chroma was negotiable; the video's was not, because nothing decodes 4:4:4 AV1 video, and that constraint left with it.

**The PQ transfer is applied once, on this side** (`tone::encode_pq`). Applying it wherever the encode happens - by libavif's caller for a still, by a `zscale` call in ffmpeg for a video - is the same curve computed twice and, worse, leaves the two encoders holding frames in *different domains* between the grade and the encode. Nothing that belongs in between could then be written once, which is what the output sharpen (§10.9) needs.

**The still is encoded in this process, by libavif** (`avif.rs`). What that library puts on the file is the nclx `colr` box - what Chrome reads to decide a still is HDR, and what ffmpeg's avif muxer does not write. Linked rather than spawned, the graded frame reaches it as a pointer instead of being written out, converted and read back: 56MB at 3840 and ~366MB at native resolution, moved three times for a picture no process wanted kept.

What ffmpeg was doing on that path was `zscale`, and it is two things already here: the PQ transfer, which is `tone::pq` - the same curve the roll-off is computed in - and the Rec.2020 matrix with its limited-range quantisation, which is libavif's own `avifImageRGBToYUV`. Nothing was reimplemented that one side or the other did not already own.

**It costs memory, and that is the trade rather than a footnote.** What child processes would hold in their own address spaces and free on exit, this process holds itself. Multiply the whole of it by `processing_concurrency`.

Measured as peak RSS - `VmHWM`, not arithmetic - building a native-resolution HDR still from the 24MP fixture, where one frame of 16-bit RGB is 145MB:

| | peak RSS |
|---|---|
| every stage allocating its own output | 1227MB |
| grade and transfer in place | 1092MB |
| + decode released once the grade has copied out of it | 1090MB |
| + all four | **954MB** |

Four buffers went, in every case because nothing else was reading them:

- **The grade** allocated its output. It is sample-for-sample at the same index, and by the time it runs the frame is one this side allocated - the warp's output, or the resize's - so `tone::grade` writes into that.
- **The transfer** would allocate another. It runs once in the graded buffer before the encoder is handed anything, with no second reader to copy for. `encode_still` keeps its `Cow` for the other half of the argument - an owned frame goes into libavif and is dropped as soon as the YUV conversion has read it.
- **The decode** stayed resident until JavaScript dropped the handle, which is after the encode - the longest stage of the job. It is owned in Rust now, and the last reader is handed it outright rather than lending it (`hdr::Decode::Owned`), so it is dropped the moment the grade has copied out and before the encode allocates anything. The 8-bit side needs nothing special: the base is built in a scope, and the decode goes when that scope ends.
- **The interleaved RGB** was held across `avifEncoderWrite`, though libavif stops reading it once `avifImageRGBToYUV` has filled the planes. It is dropped there instead, which matters because of what allocates next.

**What is left is libaom, and it is most of it.** Probed inside `write_avif` on the same frame: entering the encode is 239MB, the YUV planes add 138MB, dropping the RGB gives that back, and `avifEncoderWrite` alone then takes the peak to ~950MB. So roughly **700MB is libaom's own working set** for a 24MP 4:4:4 all-intra frame, against ~145MB of ours. It tracks pixel count rather than depth: measured as `VmHWM` over a whole job on a 61MP frame, 795MB fitted to 3840 and 2199MB at native, and twelve bits against ten is 1-4% of that. Two things follow. Copy elimination is close to done - the only frame this side still holds through the encode is the YUV planes libaom is reading. And that working set is *not* a threading trade: measured under `taskset`, two cores against eight moved the peak by under 30MB, so it is per-frame state and there is nothing to buy back by capping `maxThreads` or the tile count. Going lower means a different encoder, or an API that encodes in tiles, and libavif exposes neither.

**The still is 4:2:0, and that is a memory decision rather than a quality one** (`hdr_still_full_chroma` turns it back to 4:4:4). Dropping the still's chroma to a quarter of its samples halves what libaom has to carry, and libaom is the peak. On the 24MP fixture, everything else held - same decode, same fit, same grade, same speed:

| | wall | CPU | peak RSS | bytes | SSIM |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4:4:4, `full` at 3840 | 456ms | 1.90s | 457MB | 1.09MB | 0.9793 |
| 4:2:0, same quantizer | 330ms | 1.45s | 420MB | 0.41MB | 0.9681 |
| 4:2:0, quantizer matched to that SSIM | 339ms | 1.71s | 420MB | 1.64MB | 0.9791 |
| 4:4:4, `max` native | 1181ms | 4.03s | 960MB | 3.40MB | |
| 4:2:0, `max` native | 805ms | 3.07s | **586MB** | 1.76MB | |

Read the first two rows together and 4:2:0 looks free - a third off the clock and 62% off the file - but they are not the same picture. Held to the same combined SSIM it needs 51% more bytes.

**That combined figure oversells the damage, though, and the per-plane split is the honest version.** 4:2:0 leaves luma untouched by construction, so at a matched quantizer the Y plane is identical and the whole cost lands in chroma:

| | Y | U | V | All | bytes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4:4:4 crf 20 | 0.9747 | 0.9779 | 0.9853 | 0.9793 | 1.09MB |
| 4:2:0 crf 20 | 0.9747 | 0.9516 | 0.9781 | 0.9681 | 0.41MB |
| 4:2:0 crf 12 | 0.9898 | 0.9655 | 0.9821 | 0.9791 | 1.64MB |

So at equal bytes 4:2:0 does not lose - it *reallocates*, spending on luma what it saves on chroma, which is the trade every photographic delivery format already makes and roughly the one human vision asks for. A combined SSIM weights the three planes near enough equally and vision does not, so "51% more bytes at equal SSIM" measures the metric as much as the picture. The earlier draft of this section read that number as a straight quality loss; it is not.

What is unambiguous is time and memory: ~26% off the wall clock even at matched quality, and at native resolution the peak goes from 960MB to 586MB.

So the setting is offered as what it is - spend memory and time to hold chroma detail on saturated edges - rather than as a quality slider. Off is the default because the encoder's working set is the constraint that actually bites, and a library that would rather spend the RAM can say so.

Two things follow the setting and both have to agree: the `avifImage` format libavif is handed, and whether `target_size` forces even dimensions. That second one is not cosmetic - 4:2:0 has no odd dimensions, and only a native-resolution frame can arrive odd, since the masked-border crop takes asymmetric insets off it. `avif_still.integration.test.ts` reads the format back out of a written file at both settings, since a format that silently disagreed with the request would be invisible in the request.

**Firefox is the reason to leave it off**, beyond the memory. It plays 4:4:4 AV1 in software but will not composite it in HDR, so a full-chroma still is the one file the rewrap (§10.7.2) cannot help with - it plays, washed out. Turning the setting on is therefore a choice to serve Firefox an SDR-looking picture, which the settings copy says.

Note where the peak lands once it is on: at 4:2:0 the encode falls to 420MB, which is exactly the decode's transient, so the binding constraint moves off libaom and onto the decode (§10.4) and further encoder tuning stops paying.

Removing three transfers of a frame across a process boundary and keeping the rest in the server is the shape of the deal.

**Budget off the rendition, not off the sensor.** The native-resolution export above is the on-demand `max` path, one photo at a time. What runs `processing_concurrency` deep is the import, and that builds `full` - so the numbers to plan a machine around are these, measured the same way:

| | peak RSS |
|---|---|
| `full` at 3840 | 455MB |
| `max` at native resolution | 954MB |

There was a third row here, `full` plus the video twin at 510MB. **That 55MB was one graded frame**, the one case that could not PQ-encode in place, and applying the transfer once for both (above) had already taken it back before the twin itself went: re-measured on a 24MP frame at 3840, the two peaked identically at 338MB. **The denoise and the sharpen do not move this either**, which took work rather than luck: they run in horizontal strips precisely so their scratch does not scale with the frame (§10.9). Nothing sits on top of these figures now that no child process runs.

**A larger sensor does not cost more here**, which is worth writing down because it reads backwards. Both terms that scale are driven by the *output*: the graded frame is the rendition's size, and libaom's working set runs about 25MB per megapixel of it. The decode is the only term the sensor drives, and the bigger sensor is the one that gets halved - a 61MP frame at 3840 halves to 15MP, where a 24MP frame at 3840 does not halve at all and so decodes *larger*. Measured on the 24MP fixture, asking for an edge low enough to trigger the halving takes the decode's transient from 420MB to 178MB. A 61MP `full` therefore lands within noise of the same ~455MB, and only `max` - where the output *is* the sensor - grows with it.

**Read back rather than argued about** (`avif_still.integration.test.ts`). Everything a browser decides the file from - dimensions, pixel format, range and the CICP triple - is asserted off a written file with `ffprobe`, which is a decoder that is not ours. The first two would surface as a broken picture; the CICP would not, since a file missing it decodes perfectly and is simply not HDR, and that box is the whole reason libavif is linked here.

**The quantizer setting meant half what it said, which took a year and an HDR display to notice.** The still is handed to libavif as a quantizer *pair* rather than one number, and **libavif quantises on the midpoint of that pair**, so every AVIF this app wrote encoded at half the number the setting named. It went unseen while every file on the page came out of the same encoder; what exposed it was the Firefox video twin of the day, whose `-crf` libaom read literally, sitting beside the still at SSIM 0.9529 against 0.9802 on a 45MP frame at `hdr_crf` 20. A user reported it as blocking, noise and chroma loss in Firefox against a clean AVIF in Chrome, on one library with one set of settings.

So the encoder is given **both ends of the pair**, and every quantizer default is halved to match - `hdr_crf` 20 to 10, the two rendition quantizers 26 to 13, the two lossless ones 16 and 8 to 8 and 4. That is a rescale rather than a quality change: libavif derives the same quality from `0/2N` as from `N/N`. It is not the same file, since libaom also bounds each block's quantizer by the pair and so varies it across a frame given a range, where equal ends hold every block at the quantizer the setting names. The halved numbers are the defaults the schema declares, so a catalogue is built at them; there is no install holding the unscaled ones to bring forward.

The quantizers in the tables above are measurements taken before that rescale, so halve them to get the quantizer the encoder is handed today. The *settings* no longer name a quantizer at all: they are perceived quality, and `processing/quality.ts` is the one place that becomes libaom's number - the anchors there are what keep this rescale's conclusions attached to a setting a reader can still turn.

The lesson is one this section keeps earning: "both media are libaom now, so the setting means one thing" was **inferred from the encoder, not measured through it**. Two encoders sharing a codec do not share an API, and the API is where this was lost.

**A correction worth keeping, several times over.** This section has said the still is 4:4:4 "because it is a photograph", said SVT-AV1 "cannot be used here at all" while an SVT-AV1 video was shipping, and said both media were libaom before that was true. Each version was written from a plausible principle and each survived until somebody measured it. A claim here about what an encoder setting is *worth* wants a number beside it or it should not be stated.

**4:4:4 is not a setting, it is an AV1 profile**, and that decides the encoder. Profile 0 is 4:2:0, Profile 1 is 4:4:4, Profile 2 is 4:2:2. SVT-AV1 implements Profile 0 only and *converts silently* - asking it for 4:4:4 or 4:2:2 yields 4:2:0 with no error - which is why it could never have served the still, and why the libheif path had to name its encoder rather than leave the choice to plugin priority. libaom and rav1e implement all three; libaom is what the still uses. Measured decoder support, which is why the chroma setting carries the warning it does:

| | 4:2:0 | 4:2:2 | 4:4:4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| AVIF still, Chrome and Firefox | yes | yes | yes |
| AV1 video, Chrome | yes | no | no |
| AV1 video, Firefox | yes | yes | yes |

Firefox plays Profile 1 and 2 in software - `canPlayType` reports `"no"` for them while dav1d decodes them anyway - but plays them washed out, PQ code values shown with no transfer applied, which is what a decode that never reaches the HDR compositor looks like. So the video row is not academic even with no video encoded: it is the reason a 4:4:4 still cannot be rewrapped into HDR on Firefox (§10.7.2).

**The rule holds.** §10.4 states it as *pixels cross only on their way into an HTTP response* - and the encoders were the standing exception, three full-frame transfers across two process boundaries on every HDR still, plus a whole graded frame down ffmpeg's stdin for the video. With libavif linked and the video gone, nothing leaves except through a socket, and ffprobe is a dev-stage tool rather than a runtime dependency.

### 10.7.1 Grading scene-linear to display-referred

The decode is scene-referred, and scene-referred data carries no exposure: the decode normalises by the sensor's saturation whatever was metered. Tying linear 1.0 straight to the display peak therefore made brightness a function of the exposure rather than of the subject - measured over eight bodies, a 9.3x spread in mean brightness, with every clipped frame flat against the peak. `native/rawshim/src/tone.rs` grades the samples before they reach the encoder, and the same eight frames come out within 2.5x with nothing clipping.

Two ITU standards do the work, so no look had to be invented:

- **ITU-R BT.2408** puts diffuse white at **203 nits** (`HDR_REFERENCE_WHITE_NITS`), the value that makes HDR read at the same brightness as the SDR beside it.
- **ITU-R BT.2390** §5.4.1 supplies the **EETF**, a Hermite roll-off applied in PQ space that compresses everything above the display's peak into it rather than clipping.

Neither standard says *which* sample is diffuse white, because a camera takes that from the metered exposure and a raw file has no rendering intent. `HDR_WHITE_QUANTILE` (default 0.90) picks it from a histogram - the same heuristic dcraw's auto-bright uses - and is the knob to reach for if a library renders consistently dark or hot.

**A lower quantile renders brighter**, which is the opposite of the obvious reading: it places diffuse white further down the histogram, so everything above it scales up. The default was 0.99 and it was too high for landscape work. On a daylight frame that is half sky, the brightest 1% *is* sky and specular cloud edges rather than a lit white surface, so the anchor sat where the headroom should have started: peak 470 nits, greenery at 40. Measured on that frame, only **0.002% of pixels** are within a whisker of sensor saturation - a genuine specular tail of ~4,600 out of 60M - while p99 sits a full 1.21 stops below it. At 0.90 the same frame peaks at 823 with its greenery at 70.

The alternative would be normalising the brightest sample to the display peak, and that is precisely what the grade exists to avoid: it makes a photo's brightness depend on whether one glint happened to clip, which is what produced the 9.3x spread in the first place. Place diffuse white correctly and the tail lands wherever the scene actually put it.

**The quantile is floored at three stops under the peak** (`WHITE_FLOOR_UNDER_PEAK`). A night frame is mostly dark ground and sky, so the 90th percentile lands on the ground: measured on a lit bathhouse at night, q0.90 sat at 4% of sensor saturation with q0.99 at 59% and the lanterns clipped, and anchoring there pushed the frame 24x. The camera match then pulled the midtones back but could not reach the bottom, and the shipped render carried a pedestal at code 14-17 across the darkest third of the picture where the camera's JPEG has 0-2. Held to three stops under the peak, the same frame's darkest percentiles land within a count of the camera's. Daylight frames are untouched: across the fixtures and a set of 61MP bodies q0.90 sits between 0.8 and 2.4 stops under the peak, and an underexposed frame with no bright content keeps its peak low with it, so the floor never fires there.

The **peak is read off the frame, not off sensor saturation**, and that is what makes the grade exposure-invariant: both the white level and the peak scale with exposure, so their ratio, and therefore how much roll-off the highlights get, is a property of the scene. Anchoring the peak at sensor clip instead would give a frame shot two stops down four times the compression for the same subject.

**Both ends are quantiles, and the top one had to become one.** The peak was the frame's brightest sample, over a strided subsample - which makes it a property of *one pixel* and of how many pixels happened to be read, rather than of the scene. Two consequences, both measured on the 24MP fixture across a full decode, a half-size one and a box-resized one of the same frame:

| top-end statistic | spread across the three |
|---|---|
| maximum | **22.7%** |
| p99.999 | 0.73% |
| **p99.99** (`PEAK_QUANTILE`) | **0.29%** |

A maximum over a subsample also inherits the subsample's size: a full decode read 1.51M pixels and a halved one 379K, and the smaller read came back 0.76% *higher* because the two errors ran in opposite directions and happened to cancel. So the reading is now a fixed **1M samples at proportional positions**, and the top end is the 0.9999 quantile of them - the top ~105 samples, enough to estimate, where p99.999 is the top ten and measured less stable for being nearly a maximum again.

It clips what sits above it. That is not new - the matched arm already clamped to whatever its subsample found - but it is now explicit and repeatable rather than a function of the decode's size. Measured against the previous grade on the fixture: **SSIM 0.998**, with the per-channel means unchanged to four figures on four of the five pinned cases. The fifth is the low-peak roll-off case, which comes out 3.5% brighter, because a peak that no longer chases one specular sample asks the EETF to compress less of the picture to accommodate it - the same argument the white quantile makes at the other end.

**Renditions of one photo should agree, and now nearly do; they never will exactly.** A half-size decode is its own demosaic rather than a downscale of the full one, so resampling both to the fit grid gives slightly different planes however the statistics are read - about 0.5% on the fitted colour matrix. The goal is that nothing makes the gap *larger* than that for no reason, which is what the maximum was doing.

The curve is baked into a 65536-entry lookup table, because a 60MP frame is 180M samples and `pow()` that many times is not free. It runs **once per job**, not once per output.

`HDR_PEAK_NITS` (default 1000) is now only the declared mastering peak and the roll-off target - no longer the exposure control - so it sets how much headroom sits above diffuse white. It is not interpreted the same everywhere: Chromium renders HDR stills relative to SDR white and caps headroom at 4 stops, while Firefox 153 does no tone mapping at all, so it is a knob to set against a display rather than a value that transfers.

Three traps, all silent:

- **Colour signalling is dropped unless it is written where the reader looks.** Through ffmpeg, an AV1 encoder discarded the primaries and transfer however the `-color_*` options were set - measured on SVT-AV1, still true of libaom - so the `av1_metadata` bitstream filter had to put them back in the sequence header. Through libavif it is the nclx `colr` box, which is what Chrome reads and what the muxer never wrote. Either way the encode succeeds and the result is not HDR, which is why a unit test pins the exact CICP numbers and an integration test reads them back with `ffprobe`. Firefox's rewrap is the third instance of the same trap (§10.7.2).
- **Frames are fitted to the rendition's own longest edge**, in linear light before the transfer is applied - resizing after it would average PQ code values and darken the result. The fit is done by `reduce.slang` before the grade rather than by `zscale` after it, since grading 61MP to produce a 3840px rendition threw away fifteen sixteenths of the most expensive stage (§10.8.1).
- **The renditions outlast a request.** `Bun.serve` idles a connection out after 10s by default and the client sees a closed socket rather than an error, which reads as a crash. `idleTimeout` is raised to Bun's 255s maximum; the lossless render (§10.5) was already close to the old limit on a large frame.

**Confirmed on a real HDR Android display**, Chrome: both the AVIF still and the one-frame video render visibly brighter than their SDR references, so both paths work. Two things worth keeping from that run. `dynamic-range` reported `high` while `video-dynamic-range` reported `standard`, and the video was plainly HDR regardless: the video-plane query describes bi-plane devices like TVs, cannot be answered without knowing whether a given frame reaches a hardware overlay, and is not something to gate on. And the video looked sharper than the still until `image-rendering: pixelated` was applied to both, at which point they matched. That is Chrome's scaler, not the encode: measured against the frame both were encoded from, the still scores *better* (SSIM 0.9932 against 0.9923) at twice the bitrate. The `<img>` path filters a downscale properly; the video plane scales more cheaply and the resulting aliasing reads as detail.

**A blank video row is usually the codec, not the tagging.** Firefox on Android ships `media.av1.enabled` off for battery, and Safari has no software AV1 decoder at all; it plays AV1 only where the hardware does, so Intel Macs, M1/M2 Macs and iPhones before the 15 Pro cannot, however current their Safari. Both failed all three videos identically, SDR included, which was the tell: an HDR problem would have spared the SDR reference. Neither gap is worth working around, because both of those browsers decode the AVIF stills, which is the path that matters on their platforms; if an Apple video path were ever wanted it would be HEVC 10-bit, hardware-decoded on every Apple device.

### 10.7.2 Firefox rewraps the still itself (`avif-hdr-video`)

**An AVIF *is* an AV1 frame**, and an MP4 is another box format to put one in. Firefox composites HDR for video and only video, so the same bytes in the other container go down the path that reaches the panel: same decoder, same frame, no transcode. That is the whole trick, and once it is stated the server side of it looks absurd - it was encoding a second file per photo, at import, on disk, forever, to hand one browser a container.

So it is a **client-side rewrap** now (`packages/avif-hdr-video`, a standalone TypeScript package with no dependencies, publishable on its own). It is consumed here as source, through a Vite alias and a `tsconfig` path rather than a `file:` dependency: what it publishes is a compiled `dist`, and that is a build artefact no checkout of this repository should have to produce before the app will start. The photo view fetches the AVIF it would fetch anyway, reads the AV1 OBUs out of the item data, and writes about 800 bytes of `ftyp`/`moov`/`mdat` around them: **~1ms on a 4K frame**, against an AV1 encode per photo per import. The server has one file per rendition, no `medium` dimension through the job types and paths, and no ffmpeg in the runtime image at all - ffprobe is a dev-stage tool now, for the test that reads an encode back.

**Two details are the difference between this working and shipping a flat picture**, both found the hard way against a real display:

- **The sequence header must be copied into the `av1C` record**, not merely left in the sample. AVIF puts it in the item data and writes a four-byte `av1C` with no configuration OBUs; a player that configures its decoder from the record alone then starts with no colour description. `ffmpeg -c:v copy` from AVIF to MP4 produces exactly that, which is why the first working version was a two-stage remux through `-f obu`, and why doing it by hand is *simpler* than driving a muxer rather than harder.
- **The temporal delimiter has to come out.** AVIF item data opens with one and an MP4 sample may not contain one.

`still_picture` in the sequence header was suspected for a while and is a red herring: with the `av1C` fixed, a file that still declares itself a still picture plays and composites in HDR.

**A user-agent sniff decides whether to do it at all**, which is normally the wrong tool and here is the only one. The failure is that Gecko renders a PQ still *wrongly* rather than refusing it, so there is nothing for a feature test to catch, and `(dynamic-range: high)` answers a different question and answers it false on Firefox even on an HDR display. Everything else gets the `<img>`, which is better in every way that matters: no autoplay rules, no media element per picture, and a decode the browser can throw away under pressure.

**The package swaps the elements itself.** `install()` matches `<img>`s by selector, watches for new ones and for `src` changes with a `MutationObserver`, and replaces any that turn out to be an HDR AVIF with a `<video>` carrying the image's id, class, style, dimensions and description - so a page gets this by calling one function, and a developer who has never heard of bug 1889288 does not have to implement anything. It is a no-op away from Firefox and leaves an image alone unless the fetch confirms an HDR AVIF it can carry, so a selector that over-matches costs a cache hit rather than a broken picture.

This app does not use `install()`: React owns those nodes and the stage already knows how to show a video, so `web/src/features/photos/hdr_video.ts` takes only the URL from `hdrVideoUrl()` and hands it to the element the stage was going to mount anyway. **A twin is held for as long as its photograph is the open one**, both of them where a reader has looked at `full` and `max`, and every other photograph's is revoked - the whole MP4 is resident while its URL is alive, so the bound is the picture being looked at rather than the session. Held, because the rewrap is the whole AVIF fetched again: made per press instead, the flip between a render and the camera's JPEG - which is what the picker is for - costs that on every keystroke, and on a native-resolution rendition that is tens of megabytes each way and reads as a stage that will not move.

**What it refuses is as important as what it does.** A `grid` primary item is several coded frames the container assembles, which a one-sample video cannot express, and an encoder writes one for images past its tiling threshold, so it is a size away rather than exotic; item data stored outside the file, likewise. Both throw, and a throw leaves the `<img>` exactly as the page wrote it. And 4:4:4 does not work at all (§10.7), which is a property of Firefox's compositor rather than of the rewrap.

### 10.8 Matching the camera's own rendering (`fit.rs`)

A render carries none of what the camera would have done to the same frame: not the maker's colour science, and not the picture profile the photographer chose on the body. Buying that normally means sourcing, storing and hosting a lens profile and a colour profile per body, which is tedious where it is possible and impossible where a maker never published one - and it still cannot honour a per-shot setting. Everything needed is already inside the RAW, in the JPEG the camera put there. `MATCH_EMBEDDED_JPEG` fits the transform that takes a render to that JPEG, and applies it to every rendition built from a render - SDR here, and HDR through §10.8.1, which reuses this section's geometry and falloff as fitted and refits only the colour.

**Geometry first, colour second, and the order is not negotiable.** A colour transform is fitted from pixel pairs, and a pair means nothing unless both pixels show the same point in the scene. On a frame whose JPEG is distortion-corrected, fitting colour first plateaus at ΔE76 16 however much capacity the colour model is given - per-channel curves, curves plus a 3×3 matrix, and a 33³ 3D LUT all land within 1.5 of each other - because no tone curve can map a pixel onto a different pixel's colour. Correcting geometry first takes that same frame to 1.51.

> The ordering conclusion survives the metric problem below: 16 against 1.51 is an order of magnitude, and it is an argument about correspondence rather than colour accuracy. The parenthetical - that the three capacities land within 1.5 of each other - does not, being a small difference read off a blind measure. The corollary is that the geometry stage cannot use colour as its matching signal.

**Geometry is read where the camera recorded it.** Sony writes a distortion spline at `IFD0` → SubIFD (`0x014a`) → tag `0x7037`: an `SSHORT` array whose first element is the knot count, the knots evenly spaced from frame centre to corner, in units of 1/16384 of the half-diagonal and anchored at zero in the centre. Plain TIFF parsing reaches all of it; none of Sony's enciphered `0x94xx` blocks are involved. Validated against an independent fit of the render against the JPEG - at 28mm the spline says −2.83% at the corner and the fit says −2.78% - and the values track focal length per shot rather than coming from a table, the 28-75 zoom crossing zero near 32mm and reaching +4.5% at 75mm. Three things do not generalise and must not be assumed: the **knot count is per body and per tag** (ILCE-7CR 16, ILCE-6300 11, and within one RX100M3 file the vignetting tag uses a different count from the distortion one), **a body may record nothing at all** (of 1000 frames sampled across the catalogue every Sony frame carries a spline and only the Canons go without, which is what the lensfun tier below exists to serve), and the **vignetting tag `0x7032` must not be applied** - it describes a corner gain of +50% where the measured scene-linear ratio between the corrected render and the JPEG is flat, so applying it would inject a ~35% corner error.

**The body also says whether it used the correction, and that is worth asking before searching for one.** The SubIFD is written as flag/params pairs - `0x7031`/`0x7032` vignetting, `0x7034`/`0x7035` chromatic aberration, `0x7036`/`0x7037` distortion - and `0x7036` is 0 when the camera corrected nothing. The "on" value is not a single constant (1 and 17 both appear), so anything non-zero reads as on rather than matching a list that goes stale on the next body. It predicts perfectly in the direction that matters: across 120 sampled Sony frames every one of the 17 fits that searched and then fell back to no geometry had `0x7036 = 0`, and it never happened on 1 or 17. Taking the camera at its word skips the curve axis, which is most of what a search costs.

**It does not skip the scale, and it used to.** "The body corrected nothing" is a statement about undistortion, not about framing, and the two are separate: an ILCE-6300 with the flag off still lands on `crop≈0.996`, an EOS R8 on `0.995`. A ~0.4-0.5% rescale that survives across bodies with unrelated optics reads as a difference between the decoder's visible area and the camera's own framing rather than as a lens - measured when that area was LibRaw's, which trimmed to its own margins rather than to the manufacturer's crop (§11.1) - and dropping it cost a median ΔE76 of +0.21 on the frames the gate fired on. The scale is now fitted there too - one crop axis, no curve - which put the DSC02981 fixture from ΔE 0.6500 to 0.5634.

**Where the body recorded nothing, the lensfun database is asked before the geometry is fitted** (`native/lensdb`). It is a crate rather than a system package - a pure-Rust port of lensfun's engine with lensfun's own ~3.6MB of XML compiled into it, LGPL-3 code and CC BY-SA 3.0 data - so it is linked and not installed, and the database cannot be a different vintage on the reader's machine than on the build's. It answers for 27 of the 32 Canon frames sampled and for every fixed-lens compact, and it is cheaper than fitting from nothing because a known curve needs only a gain scanned against it where an unknown one needs the whole `k1` grid. What it buys is shape: lensfun's curves are mustache, dipping barrel mid-field before turning pincushion at the corner, which a single `k1` cannot express at any coefficient.

**The port is held against the library it ports.** Its arithmetic was compared with lensfun 0.3.99 over that release's own database, 2422 shots through both, and 2019 of the 2026 it resolved agreed to better than 1e-6 of the radius; the remainder are ties between two entries broken in a different order. What did *not* survive the comparison is its search, which `lensdb` therefore does itself (below). `native/lensdb/tests` pins the C library's answers for five shots so the port cannot drift from them.

**Second, not first, and that was measured.** Over 83 Sony frames carrying both, the body's spline beat lensfun 31 times to 4 with 48 ties, by a mean 0.034 ΔE76 and up to 0.86. The reason is in the numbers: a spline is recorded per shot, so one FE 40mm prime ranges from −0.69% to −3.09% at the corner across a session as focus distance moves it, while lensfun has one profile per lens and answers −2.10% every time. Where an entry is simply wrong the gap is larger - lensfun has the Tamron 28-75 at +0.64% where the body says +4.7%, and the body wins by 0.55.

**The database's search is scored, not exact, and the guard matters more than the match.** The search returns everything that scored above zero, ordered, so a 16mm prime is a candidate for a 150mm frame; the first entry whose focal and aperture ranges the file does not contradict is taken. That is the only part of a match checkable against the file rather than trusted. Exact matching is not an option worth having: of the 8 lens strings in one Canon library, **zero** match a database name byte-for-byte - the body writes `RF24-105mm F4 L IS USM` where lensfun has `Canon RF 24-105mm F4L IS USM`. The scored search resolves all 8 on the first hit.

**That search is ours, and three of its parts are load-bearing enough to name.** The port's own `find_lenses` has all three wrong, each silently, and each was found by comparing its answers against the C library's over a real library rather than by reading it.

- **Every entry's range is read out of its own name.** Sixty of the database's 1300 lenses state a focal range in the XML; the rest have it inferred from a model string like `70-200mm f/2.8`, which the C library does as it parses. Skip that and every range is zero, the guard above rejects nothing, and a 70-200 answers for a 24mm frame with a curve from the wrong lens.
- **A name is scored against every language the entry carries, not just its default.** 1618 of the database's names are filed under `<model lang="en">` - a Tamron reading `E 17-70mm F2.8 B070` by default and `Tamron 17-70mm F/2.8 Di III-A VC RXD` in English - and the English one is what a body writes. Scoring the default alone loses 11 of the 13 frames in this repository's Sony set.
- **The looser of lensfun's two matchers** (`LF_SEARCH_LOOSE`), which keeps an entry missing one of the query's words rather than discarding it. A body writing `F2.8` against a database that writes `F/2.8` is exactly that case.

**Knots are sampled out of a modifier, not evaluated from the stored polynomial.** lensfun's coefficients live in its own normalised coordinates, that normalisation appears nowhere it can be read off, it has changed between releases, and the ACM model uses another one again. Sixteen 1×1 calls read the mapping the library actually produces, cannot drift from it, and cost ~0.7ms - against which a resolution cache is bookkeeping. The cache is in memory and holds negatives, which is the half that earns it: without them every frame from an unlisted lens pays the full search to be told no again.

**The spline carries no overall rescale**, being anchored at the centre, while the camera also crops and rescales to keep the frame full. That one scalar is fitted, but only over the crops that *do* fill: tightest-fill is the loosest candidate and the search runs downward from it, because for barrel the camera is more conservative than tightest-fill. Nothing looser is a picture a camera could have produced, and the residual cannot be trusted to say so - a black margin is skipped by the pair gate, so a crop that leaves one scores exactly as well as the crop that fills and can win on noise. It did: across 32 EOS R8 frames, 26 were shipping a corner ratio above 1 - up to 1.0102 on a +1.73% lensfun profile, with visible black wedges where the correction had pulled the picture in.

**A known curve is applied at a fitted strength, not at face value.** Every search is now one shape - a one-parameter family of curves against slack below that family's fill crop (`fit_family`) - and the tiers differ only in what the parameter means: a gain on the knots where a curve is known, `k1` where it has to be found, and nothing at all where the body says there is no curve. Expressing crop as slack rather than an absolute scale is what makes the joint search behave, since a step along the curve carries its own fill crop with it instead of trading against it.

The gain earns its axis, because a database profile is one average of every copy of a lens and measurably not what a given body did. Over 32 EOS R8 frames the ones lensfun calls barrel wanted **more** than it states - gains of 1.3 to 3.0 - while the pincushion ones wanted about **0.4** of it. Gain 0 is in the grid deliberately: it is the rescale-with-no-curve candidate, so a curve losing no longer takes the scale down with it, which is what left IMG_0622 shipping no geometry at all when the scale alone was worth 0.22 ΔE76. Measured over those 32 frames plus both fixtures, mean ΔE76 **2.1648 → 2.0752**, best case −0.45, one regression of +0.06 where the wider grid lands the coarse scan in a different valley. Fits are ~1.7x slower.

**The family picks the neighbourhood; a measurement picks the curve** (`fit::settled_on_registration`). What a one-parameter family cannot do is bend twice, and a wide zoom does: on an RF 24-105 at 24mm the residual after the best gain flips sign twice across the radius. Nor is a family the binding constraint - the objective is. The candidate scan is scored on luma over planes blurred at σ3 on a 640px grid, which is ~28px of blur at full resolution and therefore near-blind to the few pixels of misregistration a viewer actually sees: a free `k1+k2` search scores *better* there (0.8592 against 0.8628) while measuring clearly worse under correspondence. So the winner is refined against where the camera actually put each feature. `hdr_fit::registration` warps the render through the candidate, runs the correspondence search over ~6000 points of the wide plane, and reports the mean radial displacement per radius bin; each knot then steps toward what its bin asks for, damped, and a round is kept only if the mean squared displacement falls. Sixteen knots need no family at all, so nothing constrains the shape to what a polynomial can say.

Measured as mean radial error per bin, wide-plane pixels: an RF 16mm frame goes from −2.44px at the corner to ≤0.10 across the whole radius, an RF 24-105 at 24mm from a ±1.70 double sign flip to ±0.22 inside the noisy corner bin, and a 39mm frame from +0.74 to ≤0.05. The best `k1` on those same frames leaves −3.9, −1.5 and −0.7. Over 32 Canon frames the median falls **0.35px → 0.10px**. Fits cost ~11% more wall clock: most frames take one round, and the loop stops once every bin is inside a tenth of a pixel, which is where a whole-pixel search stops being able to tell.

This is also why the prefilter is not shared. The blur exists so the camera's own sharpening and noise do not leak into what is fitted from the pairs, and on a noisy sensor it earns that - removing it costs two of 30 RX100 frames their colour transform outright. But the geometry is now measured on detail, and blurring the planes it searches costs the Canon set its 0.10px median. One constant could only pick which of the two to damage, so the falloff gets its own prefiltered grid and the geometry search reads the planes unfiltered.

**The output resamples through a cubic; the fit resamples through a bilinear.** They want different things from a filter and it is worth spending the taps in only one of them. Bilinear's softening is *radial*, which is what made it visible: the warp holds the frame centre fixed, so near the centre it lands on whole pixels and returns them untouched, while at the edge the displacement is ~23px at 3840 with an effectively arbitrary fractional part - and bilinear at half a pixel is a two-tap box blur. Sharp middle, soft edges, against a camera JPEG that is sharp to the corner. Catmull-Rom (B=0, C=½, four taps per axis) recovers a mean **+25%** of the edge gradient across six frames, +19% to +28%, and costs nothing measurable on the device. The candidate scan keeps bilinear on purpose: it is the inner loop over tens of candidates, and its planes carry no detail at the scale a sharper filter would preserve. The registration settle that follows warps through the same bilinear over planes that *do* carry detail, since a correspondence has nothing else to go on - the softening is radial and symmetric, so it moves both sides of a patch equally and biases the matched offset far less than it costs the eye.

Note this is *not* the stretch itself. Measured across 32 frames the local magnification at the edge is 2-10% on all but one (a 24mm barrel correction reaching 1.44 radially), which is far too little to explain what was visible. The filter was the whole of it.

**Lateral chromatic aberration is corrected in the same resample** (`native/rawshim/src/tca.rs`). The lens images red and blue at slightly different magnifications, so a highlight lands at a different radius in each channel - zero at the centre by construction, growing with radius, magenta on the outward side and green on the inward. On a 16mm f/2.8 frame it measures **5.05px at the corner**. Since it is one radial scale per channel, it composes with the distortion warp rather than needing a pass of its own: `warp.slang` reads each channel through its own ratio table (`image::ratio_tables`), which are identical where the lens carries no lateral correction.

**Finding it took three wrong answers, and the wrong ones are the instructive part.** An edge metric that asked how far red and blue depart from green across an edge said we were 6-10x the camera - but a genuinely coloured edge does that legitimately, and our full-resolution chroma against the camera preview's 4:2:0 produces that ratio with no defect present at all. Star centroids over 55,000 point sources said 0.03px - because saturated stars were excluded as having no centroid worth taking, and those are precisely the ones whose fringe is visible. Two wrong measurements agreeing is not confirmation. What worked was the *halo split*: magenta outward minus inward around bright stars, which grows 1.67 → 6.26 → 51.30 from centre to corner where the camera's stays flat at 0.7. A point source has no shape of its own to confuse with the lens's, and the outward/inward split keys on the one thing lateral CA does.

**Three sources, tried in this order, because they are not equally trustworthy.** The curve the body recorded for this shot (`tca::supplied_curve`); failing that, the fringe nulled around the frame's own point sources (`tca::measure`); failing that, a least-squares slope off the green gradient (`tca::estimate`). Every one of them then passes the same verification, which is what makes trusting any source safe rather than a leap. The rest of this section is those three in order, and the regression is last because it is the one that has been wrong.

**The regression is a slope, not a search.** A channel displaced by `d` leaves `R - G = -d.grad G`, and for a radial scale `d` is `(scale - 1)` times the offset from the centre - so the scale is a least-squares slope, one pass, no objective to get wrong. Three search-based estimators had answered ~0 on the frame carrying 5px, because their objective was dominated by the scene's own colour and by chroma noise. The regression is immune to both: neither aligns with the radial projection, so they add variance rather than bias.

**Measured honestly, it removes 64% of the fringe** on the worst frame (halo split +51.30 → +18.67), against 88% for the same warp driven by scales read off that frame's star halos. The gap is attenuation: the regressor is the green gradient and that carries noise, which biases a least-squares slope towards zero. Reading the frame averaged down 4x4 first recovers most of it - full size answered 0.08px of the 5.05 - but the estimate never plateaus, so there is no converged value to calibrate against and no honest way to claim the remaining 24%. **That was once claimed to be a safety property - always towards zero, so it could only under-correct - and it is false in both directions.** Measured against star halos over 36 astro frames, of the 9 it fired on 6 carried no fringe at all and were given 0.76 to 1.21px anyway, every frame with a large genuine fringe was declined outright, and where it does fire it overshoots by 2-3x. The repo's own synthetic fixture recovers 109%, 110% and 88% of three injections.

**So the shape is taken from a calibrated source where one exists.** `Lens::tca` carries a knot curve per channel, the same representation the distortion uses, so a supplied curve and a measured one are the same object downstream. A supplied curve was gain-fitted before it was applied, for the same reason §10.5 does it to a supplied distortion spline - the mechanism that caught lensfun's distortion being ~0.4x wrong on the pincushion half of this library had no obvious reason to be trusted less here. Measured, that was backwards, and the strength fit is gone with the code that did it (below). Two sources were tried.

**lensfun's TCA was measured and rejected.** Its `LF_MODIFY_TCA` sampled through `lf_modifier_apply_subpixel_distortion` covers 3 of the 5 test frames and never wins on any of them. Taken at face value it makes the worst frame *worse* rather than better - halo split +41.18, where the regression leaves +18.67 and doing nothing leaves the +51.30 above. Strength-fitted, which was the fair comparison while that path existed, it reaches +21.74. Its red curve asks for -0.07px at the corner where the frame carries +5.05px, so the fit has almost nothing to scale and correctly declines to invent it. Database TCA averages over a lens; the defect is per-copy and per-focal-length, and 3/5 coverage on top of that leaves nothing worth the tier.

**Sony's own 0x7035 was decoded and kept.** The tag beside the distortion spline in the SubIFD already walked: a count prefix of 32, then 16 red knots and 16 blue. **The values are knots of a multiplicative radial factor, `1 + c/2^21`, and are not anchored at zero.** Sony documents none of it; the constant comes from a decompile of Imaging Edge Desktop, cross-checked against darktable's shipping implementation (`cor_rgb[0][i] *= ca_r[i] * 2^-21 + 1`, knots read as `posc[i+1]` and `posc[nc+i+1]`). `spline_at` already divides by `SPLINE_UNIT` = 2^14, so what remains is 2^7. The knots are evenly spaced in radius from centre to corner, which is what `spline_at` assumes, and green carries the distortion factor alone while red and blue multiply theirs on top.

**Two readings of the same bytes were wrong before this, and both are instructive.** The first correlated the tag's raw corner value against the aberration measured off the same frames, got +0.85 and +0.78, and justified nothing: consecutive frames of one shoot carry byte-identical tags while the measured aberration on them ranges +1.69 to -0.79, so a near-constant regressor was being credited with tracking a varying target. The second rebased each block against its own first knot, reasoning that a lateral aberration vanishes on axis. It does - but these are *magnifications*, not displacements, and a channel imaged larger is larger everywhere. Rebasing discarded that uniform term and left the curve a factor of the radius out, which showed up as a measured displacement flat with radius where the model wanted one growing with it. Nothing in the pipeline objected to either: the strength fit was a signed least-squares slope and would happily scale a wrong-shaped curve, so the tier stayed plausible while making frames worse.

**The tag is applied as recorded, shape and magnitude both.** It was strength-fitted before, on the reasoning that no supplied curve deserves trust; measured over 43 frames across two lenses that was backwards, because the fit contributed the regression's own faults to a number the body already knew:

| | mean split | removed |
|---|---|---|
| no correction | 18.25 | |
| the tag's shape, magnitude refitted | 16.30 | 11% |
| **as recorded, verified** | **10.23** | **44%** |

A per-shot curve from the body that took the picture is better evidence than a slope measured off one frame. It is still verified, which is what makes trusting a source safe rather than a leap - and the verification has to evaluate the curve **at each sample's own radius**. Taking one scale off the corner and applying it everywhere over-corrects every point inside the corner, so a curve that grows with radius scores as harmful when it is the yardstick that is wrong: measured, that rejected 34 of 43 corrections that were helping.

**44% is not the ~97% the body itself achieves, and the residual looks like it is not lens aberration at all.** Chasing it turned up the following, in order of how much they are worth trusting.

The magnitude is right. Swept as a pure gain on the curve, the fringe removed peaks at **exactly gain 1.0** - 0.5 leaves 26%, 1.0 leaves 43%, 1.5 leaves 39%, 2.0 leaves 23% - so the documented unit is confirmed by an interior optimum rather than by taking the decompile's word for it.

Two mapping variants were tested and rejected. Stretching the radius so the frame corner sits inside the tag's range scored better and better on the aggregate metric, up to 60% at a factor of two, and it is an artifact: measured per radius bin, that variant *degrades* the outer frame on near-clean frames, turning a residual of -0.02 into -2.74. **The aggregate metric cannot see that**, which is why the per-bin residual exists. A half-step knot offset, the other candidate, is within noise of no change. Imaging Edge's disputed max-subtraction was dropped on inspection rather than measurement: it is a factor common to all three channels, so it cancels exactly in red-minus-green and cannot affect channel registration at all.

What the tag actually says about the lens the residual is largest on is the interesting part. Its red knots read `[3, 2, 2, 2, 1, 2, 2, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0, -1]`, which is a displacement of 0.12px at r=0.3, 0.18px at r=0.7 and **0.00px at the corner** - Sony is saying this lens has essentially no lateral aberration, and the correction doing nothing in the outermost bin is it being obeyed, not ignored.

Meanwhile the frames carry a real displacement that is **flat with radius**, and a lens aberration cannot be - it has to vanish on axis. Pooled over 33 frames of that lens the profile is +0.31, +0.40, +0.40, +0.30px across the field: a flat term of **+0.36px** and a radial slope of **-0.02**, which is to say no radial component at all. The same three Canon frames shot in daylight give the opposite decomposition - a flat term of -0.09 and a slope of **+1.54px per radius**, which is textbook lateral CA, zero on axis and growing outward. So the tier's model is right and works; these particular frames just carry something else on top of it.

**What that something is remains open, but three explanations have been tested and refuted.** It is not the demosaic: that would be identical on every frame it touches, and the daylight frames show none of it. It is not a bias in the measurement: the same nulling run over synthetic registered stars reads 0.000px at every brightness and noise level tried, so the metric has no floor to speak of. And it is not per-frame noise, which is what a single frame's profile looked like - two frames of the same lens minutes apart gave slopes of +0.43 and +0.04, which is why the number above is pooled over 33. What it does track is **astro frames specifically**, on both bodies: the one Canon astro frame carries a flat +0.33 alongside its radial +1.34, where the Canon daylight frames carry none.

The practical consequence is that the 44% is measured on the shoot least able to show the correction off. Most of what these frames carry at the corner is not lateral CA and no radial model can remove it, which caps the score whatever the curve says. The daylight frames are where the model's own domain is, and there the aberration it targets is the whole of what is there.

**Both sources still overcorrect on three of the six**, turning a +3.27 split into -11.44, and the regression does the same there. That is the estimator's problem rather than the tag's and it is not fixed: on those frames the halo metric is reading aurora structure rather than point sources, so it is not yet established whether the correction or the measurement is at fault. Related: the `MIN_SHIFT` gate declines most of the high-fringe run in that shoot outright.

**Nothing is applied without being checked first, and that check is what makes the tier safe** (`tca::improves`). The estimate is not self-checking and has been wrong in both directions: measured against star halos it fires on frames carrying no fringe and overshoots by 2-3x on those it does read. So the frame's own point sources are measured before and after, and a curve that does not measurably reduce the fringe is dropped. It costs no resample - a corrected pixel at `q` is the raw frame's red at `q * s` and its green at `q`, so evaluating a candidate is the same arithmetic with the sample positions moved.

Measured over 75 frames across five lenses, scored as the mean absolute halo split:

| | mean split |
|---|---|
| no correction at all | 22.84 |
| every candidate applied | **26.75** |
| verified | **20.14** |

**Applying every candidate is worse than applying none**, by 17%, which is what the tier did before this. Verified it is 12% better than none. Only 11 of the 75 candidates survive, and that is the correct verdict rather than an over-tight threshold - on the ILCE-7CM2 set 38 of 39 are rejected because the estimate fires on frames with no aberration to remove.

A frame with no point sources - a portrait, a flat wall - passes unverified rather than being dropped: absence of evidence is not evidence of harm, and refusing them all would disable the correction almost everywhere it is wanted. The offset the split is read at is 2px, measured rather than picked: swept from 1.5 to 10 the signal peaks at 1.5-2 and is gone by 5-6 while the standard error stays flat. It is also read at 5px, because a correction far too large pushes the channel out of the near window and the split there collapses to nothing, which reads as a perfect result - a 6x overshoot was waved through before that second reading existed.

**Where there is no recorded curve, the aberration is measured rather than regressed** (`tca::measure`). The regression above reads a slope off the whole frame at a quarter resolution, which is where point sources go to die, so it ends up fitting scene edges that carry no radial signal - it fires on frames with no aberration and declines frames with plenty. This finds the features a lateral aberration is legible on, displaces one channel until the fringe around them cancels, and reads off how far that was. The nulling is unbiased where the regression is not: over synthetic registered stars it reports 0.000px at every brightness and noise level tried, so a frame with nothing wrong measures nothing.

**The search is over a frame-wide scale first, and then refined per radius bin - and that ordering is load-bearing.** Swept independently, each bin's split *aliases*: displace a bin's points far enough and neighbouring point sources come back into the sampling window, scoring as well as the truth. Measured on a synthetic 1600×1200 field the period is 7px, and the outer bin answered a 3px injection as **5.71px**. Widening the search does not help - it makes more aliases reachable, which is exactly what widening it from 2.5px to 6px did. Searched as one scale over every point source at once, an alias needs `true + period` at *every* radius, so it sits at a different scale for each and reinforces nowhere while the truth lines up in every bin. Bins are still refined individually, because a real lens is not exactly proportional to radius, but only within half a period so the refinement cannot reach one. Injections of 3px and 5px at the corner now come back at 98% and 89%.

It fits an intercept and keeps only the slope. Forcing the line through the origin does not reject a flat term, it launders one - 0.6px at every radius comes out as a 0.93px correction at the corner, because a line through zero has nowhere else to put it. A lateral aberration is a magnification difference and must vanish on axis, so the flat part is given somewhere to go and then discarded. That discard is also the whole of the shortfall above: the innermost bin reads slightly high, the line charges it to the flat term, and about a tenth of a genuine injection goes with it. Costs 11ms on an ordinary 24MP frame and 78ms on a 32MP astro frame dense with stars.

**Longitudinal aberration is the other half, and no warp reaches it** (`image::defringe`, `raw_defringe`). Lateral CA is a magnification difference and comes out in the resample. This is the lens focusing red, green and blue at different distances: at a hard edge one channel is sharp and another is not, the channels are registered, and the rim goes purple or green. There is no geometry to undo, because nothing has moved - one channel is simply blurrier than the others.

**Which is exactly what it is corrected as.** With `σ_R = σ_G + δ` and the heat equation `∂g/∂σ = σ∇²g`, an achromatic edge imaged through the two channels comes out as

```
R − G  =  L ⊛ (g_σR − g_σG)  ≈  δσ · ∇²G
```

so the fringe is **the Laplacian of luma times one coefficient per frame**. That is not a convenient approximation, it is the defect's actual shape, and it predicts what is seen: ∇² of a sigmoid is odd, which is why a fringe reads magenta on one side of an edge and green on the other and vanishes on the flats. `measure_defocus` fits that coefficient by least squares over the whole frame before any strip is filtered, exactly as the noise estimate is measured, and `defringe` subtracts `k·∇²(luma)` from each colour-difference plane.

**The consequence is that the stage knows when it is done.** A frame with the channels in focus fits `k = 0` and comes back bit-identical - no threshold tells it to stop, the measurement simply reads nothing. A genuine coloured object contributes a *step* rather than a curvature, and objects appear at every edge polarity across a frame, so they add variance to the regression rather than slope. This is the same argument `tca::estimate` rests on, with the same failure mode: a frame *dominated* by one coloured object can still bias it.

Three guards, and the second is only correct because of the third. The coefficient is bounded, because half a pixel squared of blur difference is not a lens. Opposite signs are declined: green is the channel autofocus works on, so red and blue are both softer than it, while a red object raises `R − luma` and lowers `B − luma` at the very same curvature - so a disagreement means the scene is being read as the lens. But that veto **needs a noise band**, and shipping it without one made the stage do nothing on the frame this section cites as its worst. IMG_8408 measures blue at +0.148 and red at −0.011; the second is a fourteenth of the first and indistinguishable from zero, and vetoing on the bare product threw away a frame carrying 98.48 of fringe. A channel with nothing to say does not get to silence one that has, which is the lesson `tca::measure` learned about discarding a good channel. Third: a channel measured *sharper* than green is clamped to no correction rather than sharpened, since subtracting a negative coefficient would invent an edge rather than remove one.

**Measured on the frames this project already knew were bad.** IMG_8408 is backlit foliage against a blown sky, the textbook purple-fringing case: rendered at 0 and at full strength, the violet cast on the twigs visibly lifts and the leaves come back green, and mean ΔE76 against the body's own JPEG over the pixels it moved goes 8.615 → 8.408. IMG_0116, the 16mm f/2.8 astro frame, goes **4.731 → 3.533**. Both fit blue as much the softer channel - +0.148 and +0.234 - which is what a lens does and what no edge mask could have told you.

**Why not a gradient heuristic.** Pulling colour towards a box mean wherever the dilated luma gradient is steep, capped at 85%, has no model of the defect at all: it asks "is there a hard edge, and is this pixel's colour unlike its neighbours'?" and a thin saturated object answers yes to both, which greys out street lamps and washes the red outlines off a flame-print shirt. It also has nothing to measure, so `raw_defringe` is not a gain on an estimate but the only thing bounding the correction, globally, for every frame.

That is visible in the sweep it forced. Scored against each body's own JPEG over 214 frames from 104 shoots - the camera being the only reference that penalises *both* leaving a rim magenta and greying a lamp - the old stage's per-frame optimum came out **bimodal**: 57 frames wanted 0 and 61 wanted full strength, with the middle thinly spread. That is two populations, frames whose hard edges are aberration and frames whose hard edges are objects, and no global constant serves both. The best available compromise was 0.6, realising 71% of the benefit while visibly harming nothing - and even there the frames it hurt were a *kind of picture* rather than a body or a lens, costume and studio shoots with saturated props.

**Re-swept over the same corpus, the compromise is gone.** 215 frames, the setting scaling the measured coefficient rather than setting an amount:

| strength | visibly better | visibly worse | worst frame | best frame |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.25 | 2 | 0 | +0.18 | −1.35 |
| 0.5 | 5 | 0 | +0.18 | −2.55 |
| 0.75 | 9 | 0 | +0.22 | −3.53 |
| **1.0** | **9** | **0** | **+0.33** | **−4.19** |

Nothing is visibly harmed at any strength, full included - the worst single frame moves a third of a JND, where the old stage at full damaged seven frames and the worst moved +1.77. So 1.0 is the default, and the 0.6 compromise was an artefact of a stage that could not tell what it was looking at. The mean improvement is monotone to 1.0 with no interior minimum, which is the evidence that **the estimator does not overshoot** and needs no ceiling below 1.

**What the corpus turns out to carry** is the part no edge mask could have reported. Of 215 frames the estimate fires on 143 and declines 72 outright, and on 34 of those it fires on it clamps red to nothing:

|  | median | p90 | max | zero on |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| red | 0.026 | 0.091 | 0.260 | 34 |
| blue | 0.167 | 0.315 | 0.474 | 1 |

**Blue is the softer channel on 133 of 143.** That is a fact about the glass, recovered per frame with nothing told to it, and it is the strongest evidence that the regression reads the lens rather than the scene - the scene's colour has no reason to prefer blue. The old stage corrected all 215 frames identically and knew none of this.

Two things the re-sweep does not settle. The per-frame argmin is still split, 68 frames at 0.0 and 70 at 1.0, but it now means something milder: the 0.0 group is mostly frames whose coefficient is near zero, so both ends differ by almost nothing and the objective is flat rather than two populations pulling apart - which the tail confirms, p95 harm 0.13 at full against the old stage's 0.53. And the 18 Canon frames are flat, mean −0.004 at full with nothing helped or hurt beyond a JND; they are two shoots, so that is not a body-level finding, only a note that the stage does nothing measurable for them.

The mask went with it: no `DEFRINGE_EDGE`, no dilation, no box mean. The stage now reads a five-point Laplacian and nothing else, so its strip halo went from **10 pixels to 1**.

`raw_defringe` survives as a ceiling on a measurement rather than the amount itself, the way the sharpen blends a computed deconvolution rather than scaling a guess, and defaults to 1.
**Two defects in the estimator, found by review and reproduced. Both are now fixed, and both are pinned by tests that fail if the fix is removed.**

*Per-channel noise had a positive bias with a closed form, and it is now subtracted.* The regressor and the response are built from the same pixels: the stencil's `-4c` term and the response's `-luma(c)` share one, so their noise is correlated by construction, and green's enters the two with opposite signs. The residue was positive for **both** channels, which cleared the sign veto (that veto exists to catch things which flip sign), and being a ratio of variances it did not shrink as the noise did. A flat frame with independent per-channel grain fitted (0.124, 0.175), the blue figure *larger* than the 0.148 measured on IMG_8408, the worst real frame in the library.

For a five-point stencil the noise's contribution to both sums is computable from the per-channel sigmas: `E[curv.resp_red] = 4.v_luma - 4.w_R.v_R` and `E[curv^2] = 20.v_luma`. Both now come off, and the absolute curvature gate went with them, since gating conditioned the sample set and made the subtraction inexact. Measured after: the noise-only frame returns nothing, and a real 0.12 defocus reads **0.0926 clean against 0.0928 with the same grain on top** - noise-invariant rather than noise-driven.

Every fixture meant to catch this added the *same* grain to all three channels, which cancels in `R - luma` identically. `tca.rs` gets this right and `image.rs` did not; that is the whole reason it shipped unseen.

*Lateral aberration read as longitudinal, and the fit is now split by radius.* A channel displaced by `d` expands as `G + d.grad G + (d^2/2).lap G`, and that second-order term is the very basis this fit regresses on. It carries `d^2`, so it is positive for red and blue whichever way each channel is displaced - again exactly what the sign veto cannot see. A pure lateral aberration, achromatic source, nothing out of focus anywhere, fitted (0.054, 0.053); the coefficient was then applied at every radius including the centre, where a magnification difference displaces nothing at all. The pipeline ordering compounded it, since the defringe runs first, removes the even `d^2` term, and leaves the lateral tier measuring a half-eaten fringe.

The discriminator is that `d` grows with `r`, so a lateral confound's apparent coefficient grows with **`r^2`** where a real focus difference is flat across the field. The fit is now taken per radial bin - six, uniform in `r^2`, each needing `DEFOCUS_MIN_PER_BIN` samples - and the per-bin coefficients are regressed against `r^2` into a constant plus a slope, of which only the constant is kept. The same move `tca::measure` makes in reverse: give the part you cannot explain somewhere to go, then discard it. Residue lands at 0.008, under `DEFOCUS_NOISE`, so the floor declines it outright.

Measured: the lateral-only frame now returns nothing, a real 0.12 defocus is unchanged at (0.093, 0.091), and a defocus with a lateral aberration laid on top still reads (0.102, 0.105) - so the split is not simply rejecting everything radial. `a_pure_lateral_aberration_is_not_read_as_a_focus_difference` and `a_focus_difference_survives_a_lateral_one_on_top_of_it` pin both directions, and both fail if the `r^2` term is dropped.

One thing given up deliberately: field curvature means real longitudinal CA is not perfectly flat in radius either, so discarding the slope discards a little genuine correction. That is the conservative direction.

**What it costs and buys, over 215 frames from 104 shoots**, scored as deltaE76 against each camera's own JPEG over the pixels the stage moves:

| strength | mean d | visibly better | visibly worse | worst frame | best frame |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | -0.051 | 2 | 0 | +0.26 | -1.93 |
| 1.0 | -0.071 | 4 | 0 | +0.26 | -2.83 |

Nothing is harmed by more than a JND at any strength, the mean improvement is 40% larger at 1 than at 0.5, and the large wins appear only at 1 - so `raw_defringe` defaults to **1**.

The number worth carrying, though, is that **it fires on 37 of 215 frames**. The version before the split fired on 143. Those extra 106 were the confounds: noisy frames and frames with lateral CA, corrected for a defect they did not have. Blue is the softer channel on 30 of the 37, which is what a lens does and what no mask-based stage could have reported.

**Together they reach the body's own JPEG.** Measured over 38 frames across five lenses, as the fringe left around the frame's own point sources:

| | mean fringe |
|---|---|
| uncorrected | 34.20 |
| geometry alone | 24.80 |
| **geometry and defringe** | **5.34** |
| the camera's JPEG | 2.58 |

Geometry alone closes 30% of the gap to the JPEG; with the defringe it is **91%**.

**The metric fault below is unchanged, and it is why this section leans on the corpus sweep rather than on this number.** The failure itself belonged to the box-mean stage, which is gone - the measured stage leaves saturated objects alone, because it has a model of the defect rather than a gradient threshold. On a frame of orange street lights the old one turned them white: a small bright object is entirely beside a steep edge, so every pixel of it is pulled towards a surround that is mostly background. The fringe metric scored that as a triumph, because it only ever rewards removing colour near an edge and has no way to tell a fringe from a lamp. The stage wants a discriminator between a rim and an object - a fringe is confined to the slope of an edge where a coloured object has a core that persists past it - and until it has one, the number above is a measure of the metric as much as of the picture. The split is worth reading: on the Sony prime the geometry moves 15.65 to 15.12 and the defringe takes it to 3.45, because that lens has almost no lateral aberration and the tag says so. On the Tamron zoom the geometry does most of the work, 96.97 to 53.87, and the defringe finishes it at 8.29. Neither stage is redundant and neither is sufficient.

**The correction costs 5-7%.** Repeated A/B on a 24MP frame at native resolution: the warp is 242-253ms with a shared ratio and 258-265ms per-channel, both medians of five. The finder is 5.2-6.7ms on top, and a frame that estimates nothing keeps the shared-ratio path and pays neither.

**Candidate warps are scored by the residual they leave behind.** This is what makes the search robust: a wrong warp cannot be rescued by any tone curve, so a good score means genuine correspondence, and the number being minimised *is* the criterion the stage decides on. It needs no band selection, no subpixel interpolation and no outlier rejection. Feature matching was tried first, in four variants, and every one produced a *confident* wrong answer - three of them independently reporting "no distortion" for a frame that has 4.4% of it. The general lesson is worth more than the specific bug: **a radial error and a radial model will always find each other**, so a null result from a detector means nothing until the detector is shown to recover a synthetic injection of the effect it is looking for. An integration test does exactly that, and it earned its place immediately by catching that only the crop was being refined and never `k1`, so a 3% injection came back as 4%.

**That residual is luma, and the stage reads no colour at all.** Every question here is about correspondence - which warp aligns the two frames, and whether the best of them beats leaving the frame alone - and none of them needs to know what colour a pixel is. One luma tone curve answers as well as three curves plus a 3×3: measured over 204 frames from two libraries, an injected distortion comes back with a mean error of 0.0119 against ΔE76's 0.0134 and an identical median of 0.0075, and the held-out ΔE76 the fit finally reports lands at 1.884 against 1.889. It is much cheaper - one curve instead of three, no 3×3 solve, six fewer `cbrt` per pair - taking a mean fit from **1106ms to 613ms, 45% off**. The score is scaled by 100/255 into L\*-sized units, so `REFINE_MARGIN` and `REFINE_FLOOR` mean what they meant when this was measured in ΔE76.

**Luma is also the better-behaved signal, which was not the expected result.** A ranking has to be smooth in the geometry, and ΔE76 here is not: the 3×3 is solved unregularised, so on a low-chroma frame the channels are near enough collinear that `solve3` returns a bad row rather than refusing. On `DSC06592` the green row's sum drops from 0.966 to 0.756 between two candidates 0.5% apart in `k1`, taking the residual from 3.66 to 10.24 while the curves and the pair count barely move - a spike at the geometry that is actually correct. The luma residual over the same candidates falls smoothly into its minimum there. One frame of the 204 does come out worse under luma: `DSC06592` settles on no geometry, and the colour fitted through it is correspondingly poor. The cause sits in that solve rather than in this stage, and the stage is not the place to compensate for it.

**Colour is per-channel curves plus a 3×3 matrix, not a 3D LUT.** 777 coefficients beat a 17³ LUT and tie a 33³ one at 107k, because the vendor transform is close enough to separable that the extra dimensions only fit noise in the cells one frame never populates.

> ⚠️ **That comparison is void and this choice should be treated as unrun.** It was decided on a mean ΔE76, which is now known to be blind to the errors that ruin a render; it discounts near-neutral error, cannot see a systematic cast at all, and hides its own tail (`fit.rs`, the `deltaE` section, has the measurements). A model can lower that number while rendering a wall visibly green, and one does: IMG_8789 reports 2.47 while its bird bath and stone wall read green. The LUTs were also indexed in **RGB**, where a pure tone change runs along the diagonal and needs resolution in all three axes; a luma/chroma-indexed lattice of the same node count is a different proposition and was never tried. Re-run against ΔE2000 plus a signed per-hue statistic before quoting this.

 Curves are binned means, gap-interpolated, extended at the end slope rather than flattened (which would crush every highlight the frame happened not to sample) and forced monotone so a thin bin cannot invert them. Clipped and high-gradient pixels are excluded: the first are not invertible, and on the second a fraction of a pixel of misregistration swamps the colour difference being measured.

**Falloff is a third stage, between the geometry and the colour.** Curves and a 3×3 are position-independent and the camera's peripheral illumination correction is not, so no amount of capacity in the colour model can express it - which is why it read as an unexplainable floor for as long as the model had only those two stages. It is modelled as `1 + a r² + b r⁴` in linear light, the shape falloff actually has, so two coefficients carry it and a thin radius bin cannot bend it on its own. Fitting runs backwards rather than by search: invert the colour transform to say what render level would have produced this JPEG pixel, and the ratio of that to the level actually there is the gain. One pass accumulates both sides into 12 radius bins and a weighted least squares puts the two coefficients through them; a search over candidate gains would be one pass each. It is a ratio of summed luma per bin, never a mean of per-pair ratios, so a bright pixel weighs what it should where a pair-by-pair ratio would let a near-black one carry as much as the sky. That alternates with the colour fit three times, because the first round's curves were fitted with the falloff still in them and partly absorb it - an injected 0.65 corner comes back as 0.78 after one round and lands within 0.1 after three. Kept only where it beats the frame without one **on the pairs the round was not fitted on**, since extra free parameters can only ever look better on their own.

**It is achromatic, and that is a choice rather than an oversight.** One scalar for all three channels, fitted from luma. Real falloff carries a slight cast, which two coefficients on a luma ratio cannot express; what the 3×3 absorbs of it globally it absorbs, and the rest stays in the residual. The gain is also bounded to [0.25, 4.0], because a degenerate solve should distort the corners rather than black them out - no lens falls off by four stops and none gains.

Measured over 32 Canon frames from one EOS R8 library: median ΔE76 **1.92 → 1.61**, worst **5.01 → 3.22**, and not one frame worse. It costs **+7%** of a fit (13.99s → 14.98s over those 32 frames plus the Sony fixture, ~30ms a photo). Most of what it could have cost is bought back by keeping the stage off the geometry search - candidates only have to be ranked against each other, and a falloff common to all of them does not change that, so it runs once on the winner rather than tens of times. What the search does pay is the wider pair record, 6 bytes to 7; the gain itself is an `Option` the search leaves at None, so the lookup is a branch rather than an identity table read per channel per pair.

What corroborates it is where it declines to fire. 30 of the 32 fit a corner gain of 1.03-2.33 and the two that do not are Tamron glass on an EF adapter, which the body has no illumination profile for and therefore leaves alone - the fit reaches that from the pixels, knowing nothing about mounts.

**Sony is not excluded, it simply has nothing to correct**, and that is the same finding the `0x7032` paragraph above reaches from the other direction: the body writes a vignetting tag claiming a +50% corner and does not apply it to the preview, so a stage that measures the ratio rather than reading the tag measures it flat. Over 30 frames across two bodies and three lenses - one native FE prime and two Tamron zooms - 25 fit no gain at all and the other five land within ±7% of the identity, against 1.03-2.33 on the Canon set.

**The residual floor is content, not model.** After geometry and falloff, ΔE lands around 1 where content is smooth and 2.5-3 in fine detail, and per-tile fits show why: on one frame the smooth tiles score 0.81-1.07 while dense-detail tiles score 2.79-3.07. It is the camera's noise reduction and sharpening against our own demosaic, which no colour transform should try to reproduce - and note that blurring both images does not remove it, because NR is edge-preserving and nonlinear, so the two are not a linear filter apart. Adding model capacity for it is wasted: a 33³ LUT scores *worse* than curves plus a matrix at every blur level.

> ⚠️ Same caveat as above: "scores worse" is a mean ΔE76 verdict, on a measure that cannot see a cast. The *reasoning* about the floor being content. NR and sharpening against our own demosaic, edge-preserving and nonlinear so a blur does not remove it; is independent of the metric and stands. The capacity verdict is not.

**One fit per photo, and nothing is stored.** The fit is on the job rather than the target, so the grid tile and the full view cannot disagree about colour. Across jobs - the max-resolution export is built on demand, long after the import - the fit is deterministic, so refitting lands on the same transform rather than a second opinion, which is why no profile is persisted. A test pins that determinism, because it is the only thing standing between "no storage needed" and two differently-graded copies of one photo.

**Applied after the resize, not before.** The distortion model is in normalised radii and the colour transform is a per-pixel lookup, so the order is immaterial to the result - and warping a 60MP decode to produce an 800px tile costs seconds per rendition. Measured: 59-75ms at 800px and ~0.6s at 3840px, against 1.2-2.9s at full resolution. A test pins the equivalence.

**One decode serves the fit and every rendition.** This was the single biggest cost and it was pure waste: `writeSdr` decoded per target, so a `render` import demosaiced the same 60MP frame twice for the grid tile and the full view, and the fit decoded a third time for an identical result. Sharing one decode across the job took the fit on a 60MP frame from 3.8s to 1.9s and the unmatched baseline from 4.8s to 2.9s, so it is a win whether or not matching is on. A job whose every target comes from the embedded JPEG takes no decode and no fit at all: the targets that want a render are collected first, and where there are none the job is done - an embedded grid already carries the camera's look.

**An HDR job fits off the scene-linear decode, and takes no 8-bit one.** All it wants from this section is the geometry - the colour is refitted in the grade's own domain (§10.8.1) - and the search resizes whatever it is handed down to a 640px grid before it looks at anything. So where nothing in the job renders SDR, the render is derived from the 16-bit decode already in hand: normalised by the frame's own peak, Rec.2020 to sRGB primaries, sRGB transfer, 8-bit. It removes a whole second decode from every HDR photo, ~8% of one end to end.

It is checked rather than assumed, because the two renders genuinely differ in tone and the geometry search scores candidates by the residual they leave. Measured on both fixtures the tier and the knots come out **identical** and the crop within **0.06%**, which is ~1.4px at the corner of a 3840px frame - under the bilinear resample that follows it, and well under the median ΔE76 +0.21 the uncorrected-flag gate above already accepts. A test pins it on a lensfun-tier body and an uncorrected one.

A job has one geometry source: `fit_all` off the linear decode, whatever ranges its targets want (§10.3). **An HDR rendition and its SDR counterpart are the same picture through the same pipeline as far as the transfer**, rather than different pictures built by different code with agreement no goal of either.

It is mostly free here, and where it is not the gap is in the decision rather than the arithmetic. On IMG_5360 lensfun's curve beats correcting nothing by 0.0028 ΔE76, so which side of that the two fits land on is decided by noise: the 8-bit fit keeps the curve, the linear one declines it, and the results differ by ~11px at the corner of a 3840px frame while matching the camera equally well. The test pins the match rather than the tier for exactly that reason - on a margin that thin, pinning the tier pins the coin rather than the call.

**On by default**, because a render that does not look like the camera's own JPEG is the wrong picture, and what is left costs a fraction of the decode it rides along with. On a 61MP ILCE-7CR frame, building the grid tile and the full view goes from 2.9s to 5.3s, the added 2.4s being a ~1.9s fit paid once per photo plus ~0.4s of transform across the two renditions. Where the geometry has to be searched rather than read the fit is roughly twice that. `MATCH_EMBEDDED_JPEG=false` turns it off for an import where throughput matters more.

The lever if that ever matters is caching geometry by (lens, focal length) rather than per photo - the three 28mm frames measured agree to ~10%, so geometry pools even though colour does not - but that needs storage and is not built. Only Sony is verified; Canon records an equivalent but no CR2 or CR3 has been tested, so those fall through to the fitted path.

### 10.8.1 The same look in HDR (`hdr_fit.rs`)

The colour half does not lift to HDR, and the reasons are structural rather than approximate. The SDR curves are indexed by an 8-bit render level and answer with an 8-bit JPEG level, so their **domain stops at display white** - which is the whole of what HDR adds - and 8 bits of output is coarser than the shadows of a PQ signal, so applying them would band. **The geometry does lift**, being a property of the lens and not of a colour space, and it is the expensive half: it is reused as fitted, and only the colour is refitted in the domain the grade works in, Rec.2020 linear normalised so diffuse white is 1.0. That normalisation is what makes the curve extrapolable, which is what lets the camera's rendering stop at diffuse white and BT.2390 take over above it (§10.7.1).

**The falloff lifts too, and unlike the colour it lifts unchanged.** It is a multiplication in linear light, so it means the same thing in any linear domain - where the curves are indexed by an 8-bit level and stop at display white, two coefficients on a radius are not indexed by anything. So the SDR fit's `(a, b)` are reused as fitted, exactly as the geometry is, and `apply_lens` runs the warp and then the falloff on the 16-bit decode before the grade. Refitting it here was the alternative and would have meant inverting a transform that carries a saturation scalar as well as curves and a matrix, to recover a number the SDR fit already has.

Order matters twice over: the falloff runs *after* the warp at grade time because that is where it was measured - its radius means a position in the corrected frame - and it is applied to the render *before* the colour is fitted here, or the curves would be fitted against corners the falloff has not lifted and then asked at grade time for levels they never saw.

Measured over the same 32 Canon frames, on this section's own held-out ΔE76: median **1.96 → 1.39**, and every frame that fitted a falloff improved. The two that fit none are unchanged to two decimals, which is the check that the term is inert where it should be. Brightening corners does clip a little more of the top of a 16-bit buffer, and that is measured rather than waved at: on the worst frame in the set a 2.33× corner takes the clipped fraction from 0.089% to 0.124%.

One number no longer means the same thing on both routes. §10.8 gates on a residual measured *with* the falloff, and `hdr.rs` takes only the geometry and the coefficients from that profile, never its ΔE. The two ΔEs are not comparable and were never both about the same picture.

**Never its ΔE, and never its verdict either.** The SDR gate decides whether an *SDR render* should wear a colour transform; refused, §10.8 returns a profile with no colour on it. `fit_all` asks that same call for geometry, so gating there would refuse an HDR match on the strength of a different fit, in a different domain, against a different reference. `fit_from_preview` is the same fit without that verdict, and each stage keeps its own test - a geometry candidate has to beat the undistorted baseline, a colour has to beat the untransformed render.

**The SDR colour is kept on beating the render, not on an absolute ΔE.** A threshold of 6 stood here and was measurably the wrong test: over 62 frames it refused 17 fits that each took their frame *closer* to the camera, 9.66 → 3.11 ΔE76 on average, which is better than the mean frame it kept. The two numbers were never on one scale - the gate reads a hue-balanced held-out ΔE2000 over the fit's own pairs, which does not convert into the error a viewer sees, and IMG_1144 scores 24.4 there while still improving its frame by 40%. So `fit_colour` now returns the same measure with nothing applied beside the one with the colour applied, and the comparison is between those two. Across the same 62 frames every fit is kept and none makes its frame worse. The HDR colour has no equivalent test, which is the standing gap in §10.8.1.

**One preview decode serves both halves, and how far it was DCT-shrunk matters more than that it is shared.** Decoding the same 5-14MB preview twice - the colour fit bounded, the geometry fit in full, both landing at 640 - is the obvious alternative. Sharing at 640 was tried and rejected: a bounded decode shrinks in the DCT before it builds a pixel, so at that size it arrives barely filtered and the geometry fit is measurably worse off, costing the 35-frame set **1.654 → 1.774** mean ΔE.

That is a fact about the shrink factor, not about sharing. The colour fit now wants twice the grid (§10.8.3), so a 6000×4000 preview is DCT-shrunk only to 1500 and brought to 1280 by a real reduce - and the geometry fit resizing *that* to its own 640 is a properly filtered resize rather than a DCT approximation of one. Shared, the set moves **4.605 → 4.622** mean chroma ΔE, 0.36%, against ~90ms saved off a ~1s fit. Measured interleaved: an earlier sitting-to-sitting comparison of this same question read the share as 34ms *slower* when the same build measured 1666ms and 1739ms in two sittings, so a difference that size was never there to read.

**Which route a photo takes is not a detail, and the measurements have to take the product's.** Every rendition the service builds now goes through `fit_all`, which resolves the geometry off the linear decode: there is no 8-bit render for the other route to fit against (§10.3). The profile route is the better-fitting one - measured over the 35-frame set, mean ΔE 1.60 against `fit_all`'s 1.65, because the geometry it reuses was fitted from a full-resolution 8-bit decode rather than from an 8-bit render of the fit plane - and it is not what any rendition gets. That 0.05 is the price of one pipeline, and it was already the price an HDR library paid. The harness fits the way the product does, so the gap is visible rather than measured around.

**Below diffuse white the camera's per-channel rendering; above it, one shared gain.** Past the ceiling the whole pixel is scaled down until its brightest channel sits at the top of the fit domain, read there, and scaled back up by the same factor - so a bright orange keeps the camera's orange and only gets brighter, and a pixel twice as bright comes out twice as bright. This is the point of the exercise: an 8-bit JPEG turns everything above its clip point to flat white, and the extra range exists precisely to keep those highlights coloured.

Letting each channel run on its own extrapolation instead is what tinted the sky magenta, and it took two fixes because it had two causes. The visible one was free extrapolation, measured at Δa\* +11.8 in the top L\* band. The larger one was the **mask**: a pixel was dropped if *any* channel was near clipping, so in a sky - where blue is the high channel - every sky pixel vanished from red's and green's curves too. Both ran out of data well below the ceiling and were extrapolated from there, tinting the 75-89 L\* band Δa\* +5.2 on pixels sitting *inside* the fit domain. The end slopes tell the story: 0.435 / 0.206 / 0.336 before, 0.390 / 0.411 / 0.336 after gating each curve on its own channel. A per-channel curve needs only its own channel in range; the matrix still wants all three, being cross-channel.

There was a third cause, and it is the one that turned DSC05469's sky green: **where a channel's pairs run out is close to arbitrary, and each channel was extrapolating from wherever that happened to be.** That frame is bimodal, and measuring the mask's own decisions says why. 114K of its pairs sit below render 0.2. Its sky sits above 0.9 and is blown in the JPEG, so it is dropped - for all three channels alike, the earlier per-channel gating notwithstanding. The stretch between the two holds a few hundred pairs per tenth of the domain, most of them rejected for lying on a gradient, so whether a channel's last filled bin lands at 0.18 or at 0.66 comes down to which side of `MIN_BIN_SAMPLES` a hundred-odd survivors fall: red kept 113 pairs in the 0.6-0.7 band where green kept 13. Three straight lines drawn from three arbitrary places diverge - by diffuse white the curves read 2.19 / 1.15 / 0.79, a 2.8× spread on pixels well *inside* the trusted domain, and the held-out ΔE never saw it because that is measured only where all three channels have data. So a channel no longer extrapolates alone. **The channel whose pairs reached furthest carries the shape the others borrow above their own ends**, at the gain the two ran at where both had pairs - the channels agree on shape wherever they overlap, within about 5% across the domain on the fixture, so what a short channel lacks is reach, not a rendering of its own. Only above its own last bin: a channel keeps every pair it measured, and the borrowed part is the part that was a straight-line guess before.

That gain is read over the last 16 overlapping bins rather than at the join itself, which is the least trustworthy sample the channel has: it is the bin that only just cleared `MIN_BIN_SAMPLES`, so it is the thinnest average in the curve, and a ratio taken there is carried over the whole tail. Replayed against a channel truncated at render 0.18, the window lands the extension within 1.9-2.1% of that channel's own curve where the join bin alone gives 3.2-4.0%, and self-extrapolation gives 33-38%.

**And the gain fades out across the tail, so by the top of the domain every channel ends on one level.** A frame's brightest region is where the fit has least to go on, and on DSC05469 what it does have is unambiguous: the sky is clipped in the *sensor* to exactly (1.000, 1.000, 1.000) and the camera renders it exactly (1.000, 1.000, 1.000). Neutral in, neutral out - which three curves holding three different gains up there cannot deliver. Converged, a neutral render value maps to one number whatever channel it arrived in, and that sky reads U/V 511.9 / 512.0 against a neutral 512, at a mean saturation of 0.12.

**That shared level has to sit at or above every channel's held extension, and it is worth knowing why.** Let it sit below one, and that channel has to *fall* to reach it - so `make_monotone`, which the measured part of the curve needs because a thin bin can dip, clamps the fall flat and leaves the channel at its own level with the others at the reference's. That is the per-channel gain this exists to remove, reintroduced by the guard against a dip, and it is invisible until the two disagree about which channel reached furthest: converging on the reference's own top, DSC05469's sky read neutral off a full decode and 13% red off a bounded one (U/V 510.0 / 518.1, saturation 6.10 against 0.13). Converging on the highest held extension instead, no channel is ever asked to fall, and the same frame measures 0.14 / 0.22 / 0.12 at decodes of 2400, 3600 and native - which is the cross-size agreement §10.8 asks of a fit, rather than a number that depended on the size it was measured at.

This is not desaturation, and the distinction is the whole point: what carries a highlight's colour is the pixel, not the curve. IMG_8584's twilight sky is genuinely blue (U 524.8, V 502.3 at a saturation of 20.6) because its channels differ *in the render*, and it comes through this intact. What is given up is the fit's licence to invent a per-channel gain at a level where it measured none - and where a camera really does apply one, that is a real cost, since every channel converges on the *hottest* of the three: on the synthetic chart, built with per-channel gains of 1.06 and 0.94, the cool channel reads +2.5% at render 0.4 and +6.6% at 0.6.

**The three curves are held to one shape and a gain each - where the frame supports it, and not where it does not.** Fitted freely, the ratios between the channels wander with level, and a ratio that wanders with level is a hue that changes with brightness: on IMG_8789 green ran 15% above red through the mid-tones and came back level at both ends, so a white bird bath was neutral where the sun hit it and mint green down its shaded side, the same green on the shaded fur and on the lit edge of a brick. That wander is not the camera - measured on that frame's own greys, this body holds green to red within 1% at every level - it is our fit, each curve estimated from whatever the frame holds at each level, which at a garden's mid-tones is foliage pulling green up where nothing pulls red. The grey balance cannot see it, since the hump averages out against the ends: the neutral axis reads correct while every neutral at the wrong level is green.

But it is a claim about the camera, and not every frame supports it. IMG_9808 does not - held to one shape its channels collapse to within 2% of each other, the sky's blue lift is pushed into the matrix where it applies to every pixel including the hillside, and that frame's ΔE goes 1.99 → 4.06 with its warm hues at 9.7 against grey's 1.2. So the model is fitted both ways and the frame is asked. **Asked about drift, not ΔE**: what the constraint buys is consistency across level and a mean ΔE averages exactly that away, so scored on ΔE the constraint loses on every frame in the set including the ones whose green it removed.

> ⚠️ **This paragraph diagnosed the metric problem correctly and the fix was thrown away with the constraint.** "A mean ΔE averages exactly that away" is the same blindness described at `fit.rs`'s `deltaE` section, and drift - a per-hue spread across brightness levels - was the right instrument for it. When the chroma map replaced the shape constraint, `hold_one_shape`, the arm choice **and the drift metric** were all removed together, leaving nothing that can see level-dependent hue error. It has since come back: on IMG_8789 today the signed cast on light low-chroma content reads da\* −2.0 while the aggregate reads 0.35, because it flips sign between shadows and mids and cancels - exactly the failure drift was built to catch. Any replacement needs a signed, level-resolved statistic; a better magnitude (ΔE2000) is necessary but not sufficient.

> shape is shared by every frame (`hdr_fit::fit_curves`), because the reason it used to cost
> IMG_9808 is gone: the per-channel behaviour it gives up now has somewhere else to live. The
> lattice gained luma-to-chroma and chroma-to-lightness terms, so it is indexed by chroma *and*

 Drift is measured per hue as the spread, across brightness levels, of the transform's log ratio to the camera against green - per hue so that a frame merely containing dark blue and bright yellow does not read as drift, against green so that a difference in exposure drops out, and over every pixel the camera did not clip rather than over the fitted pairs, since the fit's mask also drops everything above `TRUST_CEILING` and that is where free curves go furthest wrong.

**Whichever measures less, with no margin favouring the constraint** - and the margin that was there is worth recording, because the reasoning for it was sound and the answer was still wrong. The constraint describes something this camera really does, so it looked like it should survive a near-tie, and IMG_8789 is a near-tie at 0.130 free against 0.134 held. But held, that frame renders a deep blue pot violet: green cut to a 0.151 share of the pixel against the camera's 0.284, a 36° hue error on a saturated object, by the same mechanism that costs IMG_9808 its hillside. Measured region by region against the camera - bird bath, brick, pale fur, pot - the free fit is closer on three and level on the fourth, the fur the margin was protecting differing by 0.0008 either way. The whole-frame drift the margin was justified by is dominated by lawn. Across 35 frames the mean ΔE against the unmatched-curve baseline goes 2.03 → 1.60.

The choice invites the question §10.8 asks of any fit - whether it survives a change of decode size - and it does: over decodes of 2400, 3600 and native, every frame checked keeps its arm, and the ratio between the two measurements moves by a few percent rather than across the point where it would flip.

One shortcut here is worth naming because it looks obviously right and is not: asking the question of the round-one curves alone, rather than building both models in full. Drift is a property of the tone stage - a matrix is linear and a saturation scalar is one number, so neither can make a colour depend on level - but what the constraint *does* to a frame only shows once the matrix has been fitted around it and taken back out of the target. Measured before that, IMG_9808 reads 0.105 held against 0.110 free and keeps the constraint that ruins it. Four of the 35 choose differently.

Nothing rebuilds a rendition on a change like this: a photo is reprocessed when its pixels change or the user asks, so libraries carry HDR renditions made by whatever the fit did the day they were written.

**The case is covered by a chart the test builds rather than a frame it reads.** All three channels of the DSC02981 fixture fill every bin, so both extension paths run empty loops on it and the grade pin is byte-identical either side of this fix - which is also why `the_three_channels_leave_the_fit_domain_at_comparable_levels` never caught the green sky. `warm_chart` synthesises the shape instead: flat patches, since the mask drops anything on a gradient, most of them in the domain all three channels share and the rest warm enough that only red carries pairs past it. Because the chart is rendered by a known curve, the test can hold the fitted result against the rendering the camera *would* have made above the join, which no real frame can supply: the borrowed tail lands within 0.3 / 0.7% of it at render 0.4 / 0.6, where extrapolating per channel is 8.4 / 19.2% hot and climbing. Only that far up: above it the convergence takes over by design, so a chart built with a real per-channel gain in it is the wrong thing to hold the top of the curve against, and what governs there is neutrality instead. The chart's warm patches stop at render 0.45 for a reason worth knowing: rendered, a patch at 0.62 against green's 0.20 leaves the sRGB gamut, and an 8-bit preview cannot hold it.

**A knot is a line through its window, read at the knot, and never the window's mean.** The bins are evenly spaced in the fit's linear domain and a camera's toe is not: across the two or three bins a knot averages, the camera's answer trebles, so a mean lands near the top of the window rather than at its centre and the curve sits above the very bins it was drawn from everywhere the shadows are. Measured on a backlit 61MP frame, knot 3 came back at 0.0101 where its own bins held 0.0060 - the shipped render sat 7 L\* over the camera's at L\* 10, 4.5 at L\* 15 and 1 by L\* 27, its darkest crop 15 counts of 255 light, and it read as blacks lifted and colour drained, at 60% of the camera's chroma in the shadows, a pedestal desaturating what it lifts. The softening above the knots is a mean of the same kind, so what it averages is each neighbour's line *read at this knot* rather than the neighbour's own answer, which puts most of the lift straight back. Held-out ΔE over four frames: 2.80 → 1.61, 2.35 → 1.12, 1.13 → 0.86, 3.54 → 2.51.

**The 3×3 is weighted perceptually, and that is not a detail.** Unweighted least squares in linear light is dominated by the brightest pixels - on a frame that is half sky, the sky *is* the fit - and the matrix it lands on oversaturates everything darker, measured at 1.093× the camera's mean chroma. Weighting each sample by d(∛v)/dv, so it counts for its perceptual size rather than its photometric one, takes that to 1.042. The last 4% is a saturation that varies with level, which a 3×3 structurally cannot express, so one fitted scalar blends towards luma.

**It is also weighted by hue, because what a frame is *of* must not decide what the camera is taken to do.** IMG_8789 is a lawn with two brown dogs on it. Minimising over every pair equally lands within ΔE 2.96 of the camera and still renders the dogs olive, because they are a small enough share of the pairs that their error costs the fit almost nothing - and the held-out ΔE cannot see it either, reading 2.961 against 2.995 across settings that move the dogs by three levels. So a pair counts for the reciprocal of how common its hue is, bounded either way so that forty magenta pixels cannot take over from forty thousand green ones.

> ⚠️ Note what this is: **a workaround for a blind metric, not a fix for the blindness.** Reweighting the pairs makes the rare hues count more, but the measure still cannot see a cast, still discounts near-neutral error and still reports a mean. On this same frame, hue balancing in place, the fit today reports 2.47 and renders the bird bath and stone wall green. Where the weighting *does* earn its place is that it is the only reason rare saturated content constrains the fit at all - keep it, but do not read it as having solved what this paragraph describes.

 Hue is read off the camera's rendering rather than the render, that being the thing matched, and everything too close to grey to have a hue shares one bucket.

**The damping is chosen by the frame, on the pairs and the picture together.** A fixed ridge cannot serve both ends of what arrives: a frame whose colours all sit near the grey axis cannot constrain nine parameters and will fit its own noise without damping, while a frame that *can* constrain them does not get to - IMG_8789's camera scales green by 0.884 on grass and 0.690 on a brown, only the off-diagonals can say that, and damped to near-identity they cannot. So the identity and six ridges are tried and each is scored twice, and the one with the least *sum* wins. The pairs are asked hue-balanced, that being the question the balance exists to ask. The picture is asked by area, because a picture is seen by area: on IMG_9808, sky and snow with no colour spread to pin anything down, the least damped candidate returns a row of `[-0.285, -0.334, 1.619]` that reads as an improvement hue-balanced while the hillside renders acid yellow-green and the frame's own ΔE doubles, and the sum is what says so. A sum rather than a margin the picture may refuse a candidate by, because a refusal is a cliff: a frame a hair past the margin loses the whole matrix and lands on the next rung, which may be the identity, and two near-identical frames come back as two different photographs. Summed, the picture's loss is weighed against the pairs' gain in the same units and the choice drifts as the frame does. Removing the search entirely costs the set's mean ΔE 1.74 → 1.90, and IMG_8789 3.46 → 4.92.

**"By area" is measured on the picture, not on the pairs, because on some frames the pairs are not a sample of it.** Every gate in `fit_pairs.slang` exists to find pixels the camera can *teach* from, which is far narrower than the pixels a fitted transform is *applied* to - a pair needs all three camera channels between crushed and clipped, and a frame lit by one narrow hue fails that almost everywhere. DSC02718, a stairwell under orange lights, has the camera's blue crushed on 89% of the grid: the matrix was solved from the white-lit floor and the green grating, a near-neutral 11%, and then run over the other 89%, where it added blue the camera never rendered and the picture read pink. Held-out pairs cannot object, being drawn from the same ninth. So the mask carries a `FRAME` bit beside `COLOUR` - one pixel in `FRAME_STRIDE` along each axis, gated on nothing but the warp's black margin - and a second compaction over that bit gives the picture's score a set of its own. What the pairs reject is exactly what it keeps: a crushed channel is a poor thing to solve against, being a quantised bound rather than a level, and a perfectly good thing to be wrong against.

**The objective can see past the sRGB hull, and the frame set is where it looks.** The camera's rendering is an sRGB JPEG, so a ΔE against it reads zero for anything past that hull - `fit_score.slang` clamps to the gamut before Lab, and a target the camera clipped is no level at all. That is blind in exactly the direction a matrix with a large negative off-diagonal errs: on DSC03422's red hood the camera renders sRGB (181, 0, 1) and the fit's choice had green at a thousandth of red in Rec.2020, a hundredfold past the hull's tenth, with every score identical on it. So a pixel the camera clipped stays in the frame set, marked by a negative balance, and is scored for one thing only - how far past the hull the candidate renders it, as the fraction of chroma a luma-preserving pull to the hull would take off (`excursion`), weighted into ΔE units by `GAMUT_WEIGHT`. Measured on that frame, no ridge candidate can bring the hood inside the hull - only the identity does - so the weight is set for the sight it gives rather than tuned to that hood; the grade's floor is what holds the hood (§10.7).

The identity is a candidate like any other, since the most damped ridge is still solved from the pairs and can contradict the picture too; it wins where its sum is the least. On the stairwell the frame's own ΔE runs 11.9 for the identity to 31.8 for the least damped ridge, where the pairs' score ran the other way, 13.9 down to 11.1; the near-identity wins and the lights render orange. The lattice's rungs take the picture's score into the sum a rung has to beat, because the lattice is taught on the pairs too and a node they never reached extrapolates onto the pixels they left out.

The fixtures do not move: DSC02981's least damped ridge wins on the picture as well as the pairs, so the grade pin is unchanged. That is the shape to expect - this refuses a candidate rather than choosing one, and on a frame whose pairs *are* a sample of it there is nothing to refuse.

**The saturation scalar is fitted against ΔE, not solved from a mean chroma ratio.** The ratio is a proxy, and it fails exactly when the stages above leave a residual: one scalar can always be found that makes a mean come out right. On IMG_9808 the one that did came out at 1.153, taking hues already within ΔE 1.3 of the camera and pushing the hillside to 11.6 while the number it was solving for looked perfect. Fitted on the error itself by golden section - the objective is a sum of distances to a point moving linearly in this scalar, so it has the one minimum a search needs - it lands at 0.995 there and leaves frames whose chroma really is short alone. Every pair counts the same for this one, unlike the curves and the matrix: those can act per hue, a single scalar moves the whole picture at once, so balancing it does not protect a minority hue but hands the picture to one. Balanced, IMG_9808 lands at 0.772 and drains its sky - 75k pairs at ΔE 9.4 - to bring 1.2k red ones in.

Together: held-out ΔE 2.26 on the test frame, contrast and saturation both within 0.2% of the camera's.

Two performance traps, both measured on a 61MP frame. **Warping before downscaling** took the fit from 1.6s to 17s, and the SDR path already had the order right: down to twice the fit grid first, so the warp resamples prefiltered pixels rather than aliasing on the way in. And in the grade, **returning tuples per pixel** cost more in collection than all the arithmetic - 180M allocations - so the matched loop is flat and scalar, with a per-channel lookup for the below-ceiling case that covers nearly every pixel of a photograph. Grading with the match is the price of a cross-channel transform - there is no single input level to key a lookup on - which is why the resize below matters so much: at 3840 the whole rendition is 1.7s matched against 0.9s not, where at native resolution it is 8.1s against 2.9s.

**Fitting the model twice and searching two parameters made the measurement itself the cost, and the fix was to stop recomputing what does not change.** The fit measures its own error about a hundred times per photo - every ridge candidate at every round, every step of the saturation search, both arms of the shape question - against once for a single-pass fit, which is the difference between 119ms and 1058ms on a 24MP frame. Everything below is measured on that frame at six threads, which is what a worker gets when several run at once rather than the whole box, and **every step of it leaves the graded output byte-identical**: the fixture pins and all 35 frames of the comparison set hash the same either way.

- **A probe only moves one stage, and the stages under it were being redone anyway.** A ridge candidate moves the matrix, so the tone curves above it are the same for all six; a saturation probe moves only the blend, so the matrix under it is the same for all thirty. Keeping each and handing the finished colour to the scorer: **1058 → 476ms**.
- **The camera's target was re-derived on every pass**, three `powf` and three `cbrt` per pair, for a Lab value that never moves; and `rec2020_to_srgb` was being rebuilt *per pixel per pass*. `fit.rs` had already solved half of this for the SDR fit - `linear_table` and `lab_from_levels` exist because "the transfer's pow() is the most expensive arithmetic in the fit" - so the HDR path uses them rather than its own `delta_e76`.
- **Quantising to an 8-bit level is a search, not a transfer.** `(255 · oetf(v)).round()` steps at 255 fixed linear values, so the `powf` becomes a lookup: **476 → 357ms**. Those edges are walked to the first f64 the transfer really sends to each level rather than taken analytically, because the two do not round-trip exactly and a level out by one is a different measurement. Starting from a guess indexed by `√v` instead of bisecting saves a further **83ms** - two dozen unpredictable branches per pair costs more than a square root.
- **Probes that do not depend on each other are asked together.** One at a time, a frame's 47 blocks over six threads leave most of them idle in the last round and the fit reached only 4.2×; batched, the six candidates and the twenty sweep steps keep every core fed, and the three tone curves fit at once. The block size cannot be shrunk to fix that instead - it is part of the summation order, so it is part of the answer.

- **Only the winner's saturation is kept, so only the winner's is fitted.** The search is the fit's most expensive stage and a third of it was going into a model about to be discarded. Deferring it past the arm comparison does move that comparison, which no longer sees the blend - four frames change arm - and all four come out closer to the camera for it, the set's mean ΔE 1.628 → 1.601. That reads as the cleaner question rather than luck: drift is about what the tone stage does *across levels*, and a scalar on chroma is the same number at every level.

**Wall time is not the measure here; CPU is.** Renditions are built several at a time, so work moved onto another core is not saved, and a change that halves wall while doubling CPU is a throughput loss. Measured on the same 24MP frame at one thread, which is what the fit costs a machine: **1590 → 864ms against main's 118ms**, and across a whole rendition **12.4s → 12.9s of CPU, +4%**, for wall within noise of unmatched (3.10s against 3.12s). Running the two arms under `rayon::join` was tried and measures 98ms *worse* even on wall: the batched probes already fill the cores and nesting only costs locality.

What remains is not overhead but the work itself - 84% of the fit's CPU is the probes, 67 passes over a quarter of a million pairs, where main makes one. Cutting that further means cutting probes, and the obvious candidate was measured and rejected: running the ridge veto only on the final round saves 249ms of CPU and costs the set's mean ΔE 1.601 → 1.657, which is a bad trade for 1.8% of a rendition's CPU.

Memory goes 627 → 673MB on a 24MP frame, all of it fixed-size buffers on the 640px fit grid rather than anything that scales with the picture - on a 61MP frame, where the decode sets the peak, it is unchanged.

The blocks are summed in a fixed order rather than reduced, and that is load-bearing: floating point addition is not associative, so a reduction whose tree follows `rayon`'s scheduling gives a different fit run to run, and the graded output is pinned by hash.

A third that was pure bookkeeping: the fit **normalised the whole decode to diffuse white before resampling it**, which is a frame-sized `f64` copy - 1.46GB on a 61MP photo - built only to be box-averaged down to the preview's grid on the very next line. The divide belongs inside the resample, where it costs one multiply per sample already being read.

**The pixel stages run across cores.** The resize, the warp and the tone map are all per-output-row independent, and they were the one part of the pipeline still single-threaded while `rayon` sat linked for the SDR geometry search alone. Measured on a 24MP frame, the resize-warp-grade of a 3840px rendition went 270ms to 87ms. `warp` itself is deliberately left sequential: it runs *inside* the fit's candidate scan, which is already parallel.

**Where there are two encodes to overlap, this is the shape.** Only one is written today (§10.7.2), but the pattern is worth stating: one graded frame serves both media and the two encoders run concurrently under a `thread::scope`, both reading that frame and neither writing it, so the pair costs about what the slower of them costs alone. Sequentially they regrade the frame for the second *and* serialise the encoders - measured on a 24MP frame at 3840, in-process CPU 649ms against 331ms while wall time barely moved. The saving is in CPU rather than wall clock, which is exactly what an import queues for at `processing_concurrency` workers, and the same reason a stopwatch on one photo cannot show it.

**Resize, then the lens, then grade** - in that order, and each position is load-bearing. The resize comes first because grading all 61MP and then fitting to 3840 discards ~15/16 of the most expensive step; doing it in linear light here instead is the same picture, and is the difference between ~7s and 1.7s on a full-size rendition. The lens stage - the warp, then the falloff - comes next because both models are in normalised radii, so applying them to 61MP to make a 3840px rendition is sixteen times the work for the same result. The grade comes last because the colour was fitted from pairs that only correspond *through* that warp and only carry that falloff.

That ordering costs one thing if the levels are read naively: averaging pulls a specular peak in, so a downscaled copy reports a different diffuse white and a different scene peak from the frame it was made from. Reading both ends as quantiles over a fixed sample count is what answers it (10.7.1) - the anchor does not move with the frame's resolution, so it can be measured wherever the frame happens to be, and the decode is free to arrive already fitted.

**A colour match always carries the lens it was fitted through.** `HdrMatch` holds a `Lens` and an optional colour transform. Lens-only rendering is valid, while colour without its geometry is not. A stored lens-only analysis can be completed by fitting colour through that lens. Cached scene peaks are read and written only when colour is applied, because the peak is downstream of that transform.

The falloff repeated the lesson at one remove, which is why the `Lens` exists rather than three parameters. Threaded as `distortion`, `crop` and `falloff` down two call chains, the two routes that build a match each had to remember all three - and the one that fits both halves at once promptly did not, dropping the falloff with every test still green. *Applying* half was unexpressible; *fitting* half was not. One struct closes both.

**Every job requires `cameraMatch`.** Its values are `none`, `lens`, and `lensAndColour` in both TypeScript and Rust. The worker forwards the value into the native command, and tests cover the complete mapping as well as which parts the renderer applies. A missing value is a protocol error.

**Verification stops at the signalling.** `ffprobe` confirms BT.2020/PQ/BT.2020-ncl and 12-bit on the still, which `hdr_media.integration.test.ts` does on every run. Whether any of it lights up a panel is not observable from script: the frame goes to the compositor, and anything read back through a canvas has already been tone-mapped. A page putting six renditions side by side answers that on real hardware once, and then keeps a second encode path alive for as long as it exists to keep asking - so there is not one.

### 10.9 Denoise and sharpen

**The denoise runs on the mosaic, before the demosaic, and it is a different implementation from everything else in this section.** It is documented in §10.9.1; what remains here are the two corrections that genuinely belong after a resample - the defringe and the sharpen, both off at 0, and both applied only where the RAW is actually **rendered**. A grid tile made from the camera's embedded JPEG gets neither: the body has already sharpened it, and doing that again is doing it twice. The defringe is a lens correction rather than a cleanup and is documented in §10.8, but it runs here, first, as one of the two stages `image::finish` carries.

`raw_defringe` is a library setting because it is a ceiling on a per-frame measurement. The sharpen is not: how much deconvolution a photograph wants depends on the photograph, so it is `EditDoc`'s `sharpening`, beside the Detail panel's denoise pair.

There are no library-level denoise settings. A strength belongs to the photograph rather than to the library - a frame at 12800 and one at base ISO want different answers - so it lives in `EditDoc`, as the Detail panel's two sliders. What follows about *where* the stages fall is independent of that and holds for all of them.

**The stages straddle the geometric warp, and which side each falls on is measured rather than chosen.** The defringe runs on the coded base *before* the warp and before the fit; the sharpen runs after the warp and the fit-to-size, on the frame at the size it will be encoded at.

The two that go first do so for two independent reasons that land in the same place.

**The fit has to see the frame it will be applied to.** Fitted against the raw render instead, the colour transform is calibrated on colour the denoise then removes and nothing puts back - 22% of mean chroma, measured against the match alone - and the lateral tier measures a fringe the defringe also removes, so the two correct it twice. Reversed on IMG_8408 that overshoots from 4.22 to 84.44; fitted on the finished frame the lateral tier finds nothing left and declines by itself, and the fringe lands at 1.17 against 3.53 for the old arrangement.

**And the noise is the sensor's, which the warp stops being true of.** Noise is generated at the sensor and so is spatially uniform in sensor space; the warp resamples non-uniformly by radius and breaks that, while a global median applies one sigma everywhere - an estimator for a uniform field. Measured on a synthetic flat field carrying uniform noise, median absolute high-pass on luma per radial band, corner against centre:

| | centre | mid | corner | corner/centre |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| input | 69.8 | 69.7 | 69.7 | 1.00x |
| after the warp alone | 63.2 | 47.4 | 48.0 | **0.76x** |
| warp, then denoise | 18.5 | 10.6 | 10.9 | **0.59x** |
| **denoise, then warp** | 12.0 | 8.8 | 9.0 | **0.75x** |

The warp destroys uniformity, and denoising after it compounds that: one global sigma over-denoises the radii where the resample has already suppressed noise. Denoising first adds nothing to the warp's own non-uniformity and removes more noise besides. A synthetic field rather than a photograph because on a real frame the radial profile is mostly *scene* - a subject in the middle reads as noise to any high-pass - and a first attempt at this measured the composition. Two things to keep honest: on a real frame the two orders differ by mean ΔE ITP 0.360, under the visibility threshold for most pixels, so this is a correctness fix to the estimator rather than a visible transformation; and it stops the denoise making noise less uniform rather than making it uniform, which would need a spatially varying sigma.

The sharpen goes last because it is a Richardson-Lucy deconvolution *of the resample's own blur*. Ahead of the warp it inverts a point spread that has not been applied yet and is then softened by the warp that applies it - measured at 17.68 acutance after against 16.38 before.

**Splitting them is also cheaper than one pass**, which is the opposite of what an extra deinterleave suggests. Run together, the sharpen carries the chroma denoise's radius-32 halo through `strip_interior`, which cuts the strips far shorter than the deconvolution alone needs and multiplies the seam work every strip discards. Measured on a 24MP frame, medians of three:

| | wall | CPU | peak RSS |
|---|---|---|---|
| one pass, all three | 4.99s | 32.4s | 316.3MB |
| **split** | **4.43s** | **26.9s** | **316.5MB** |

**The slot ahead of the warp is perceptual without being the grade's output**, which is what makes one arrangement serve both the renditions and the editor. Every stage here reads a *difference* against a blur, and a difference taken in linear light follows absolute luminance rather than what the eye reads (below) - measured, linear flattens shadow texture by a factor of forty. But the filter never needed the *grade's* output either. It needed a perceptual domain, and PQ against the frame's own diffuse white is one that has nothing to do with the exposure. Held against filtering the graded frame, the mid-tones - 29.5M of a 29.6M-sample frame - land within 20 counts of 65535 and the surviving grain matches within a few percent.

**So the base is coded into that domain once, and nothing below it converts.** `tone::encode_base` puts the decode into normalised PQ - `pq(level * reference / white)` at 16 bits - immediately after the levels read the anchor off it, and every stage from there to the shader is pointwise on what the buffer holds: both filter passes, the fit-to-size, the warp, the downscale. The shader undoes it with a 65536-entry table (`decode.slang`), which is the same table for every photograph because the coding anchored the frame before applying the curve.

The arrangement before this handed each pass an `image::Coding` that converted inside `deinterleave` and `recombine`, so no second frame was ever materialised - but the *arithmetic* still ran per sample, at two `powf` on the way back, and it ran once per pass. Two passes on a 24MP frame measured 400ms of a 2650ms job (§10.3). Coding once is one table lookup per sample, total.

Three consequences worth stating rather than discovering:

- **The falloff is not a multiply in the coded domain.** It is a multiplication of *light* - vignetting is an optical attenuation - so `warp.slang` applies it with the multiplication folded into PQ's own intermediate: `y' = g^m1 · y` cancels the `^m1`/`^(1/m1)` pair, leaving two `pow` where a round trip would take four. The `g^m1` depends only on the radius bucket, which is a `u8`, so it is tabulated per warp rather than per pixel. A gain of exactly 1 returns the sample untouched, because the fold is `pq(pq_inv(u))` there and that is a count out on some codes.
- **The averages that remain are in the coding, not in light.** `Cut::downscale` and the editor's pyramid are one kernel - `reduce.slang`, an area average over each output pixel's own fractional footprint - and it averages codes. A mean of linear levels is what a lower-resolution sensor would have integrated and a mean of PQ codes is not; it is taken there because the alternative is the two whole-frame sweeps this exists to remove, and because the downscale is a grid tile in 99 cases of 100. The draw's own taps *do* decode first, so a 1:1 view is exact.

  **A pyramid level is that kernel at a ratio of exactly two**, which is what makes a canvas zoomed out and a rendition cut to the same size the same picture; `the_pyramid_is_the_resize_at_a_ratio_of_two` holds the two bit-equal over every level whose axes are still halving. The pyramid exists at all because of what it feeds rather than because it averages differently: a rendition's output shrinks as the ratio grows, so one pass reads the source once at any depth, while a canvas is fixed-size and taps per output pixel grow with the zoom - 9ms for one tap against 71ms for sixteen on a 61MP frame. Building it at the open is one linear pass over 1.33 frames, and leaves each draw sampling a level where four taps is the whole answer.

  **The fit-to-size is not one of them, and finding that out cost a wrong claim.** It happens inside the decode, in linear, before the coding - and `hdr::graded_as`, the entry the pins and the examples grade through, was coding first and resizing after. So the pin was measuring a PQ box average production never performs. Reordered to match, every row of `hdr_grade.pin.txt` returns to within 0.08% of what it read before any of this branch: the largest is the matched 3840 blue mean at 0.075%, and the neutral rows are identical to three decimal places. What that says is that the *coding* moves the picture by nothing measurable, and everything the pin had recorded as movement was the resample being done in the wrong domain.
- **The `u16` lands better than it did** in principle - linear spends its codes where the eye cannot see them, and a frame whose diffuse white sits near level 6000 of 65535 has almost none left below it, where normalised PQ is near-uniform in what a reader can distinguish. The pins do *not* demonstrate it: the graded black floor appeared to drop from 69 counts to 17, which looked like the argument and was the PQ resample above. Reordered, it reads 69 again.

PQ's range ends at 10000 nits, so a level past 49x the frame's own diffuse white saturates in the coding. That ceiling is not new: the `Coding` the filters borrowed clamped at exactly the same place, so any frame reaching it was already losing those levels in the denoise. It is BT.2408's own headroom above a 203-nit white.

That is also what lets the editor's tick be the grade alone, 16ms against 645ms: the filter runs once at open rather than per slider position, and a rendition cuts every size it needs from one filtered base.

The **colour** half of the camera match still fits against the untouched linear plane. That is tolerable for one reason: `fit_source::prepared` box-averages the decode down to the width the preview is fitted at, which already removes most of the chroma noise a denoise would have. It is a smaller gap, not no gap.

Two stages, and the order between them is not interchangeable either:

1. **Defringe**, first, because its regressor is the curvature of luma and its coefficient was fitted against the frame's luma over the whole of it. Correcting against a differently-filtered one would apply a coefficient measured in one currency to a curvature denominated in another.
2. **Sharpen**, a Richardson-Lucy deconvolution of luma.

Both run on perceptually-coded samples, never on linear ones: a difference taken in linear light is proportional to absolute luminance, so it treats a highlight and a shadow completely differently, and both of these read differences. There is one coding and they share it - normalised PQ against the frame's own diffuse white - so neither converts anything. The defringe runs ahead of the warp on the shared base. The sharpen runs on a render job's largest cut, after its fit-to-size and warp have applied the blur it deconvolves and before any display's roll-off. Smaller targets in that job are downscaled from the sharpened cut.

The denoise is upstream of all of this, on the mosaic, which is not a rearrangement of the order above but a different place in the pipeline entirely (§10.9.1). "Denoising first" was already load-bearing for the sharpen - Richardson-Lucy inverts grain as readily as blur - and it is now first by a wider margin than it was.

**The shared stages and the cut have different scales.** Denoise and defringe run once on the decoded base. Sharpen runs after the largest target in one job is resized and gathered. The import job's `full` target is therefore sharpened at 3840 and its grid target inherits that result through a later downscale. An on-demand `max` job has one native-size target and sharpens at native size. A job carrying any other smaller target still reuses its largest sharpened cut for the rest.

**A rendition is not bit-identical across machines.** The box mean's vertical pass bands by core count, so its running sum accumulates in a different order on different hardware - measured, 16-27 samples in 300,000 differ by one count between 1 and 16 threads. Nothing in-process depends on it (the AVIF differential compares two routes in one process), but an on-demand `max` export re-rendered on another machine will not hash the same as the one taken at import.

**Two caveats on the constants, both worth knowing before trusting them.** They were tuned on an sRGB rendition, and two of them - the coarse chroma cap and the chroma `eps` - are absolute fractions of full scale, which sRGB and PQ do not share: the HDR pair gets the same numbers and nobody has measured whether they are the right ones there. The sharpen measures capture blur where a frame supplies enough edges and composes resample blur only as the output shrinks. Without that measurement it uses the fixed fallback sigma; at `max`, where no resize contributes a point spread, that fallback remains an unmeasured assumption.

#### Why not the guided filter

He, Sun and Tang's guided filter fits `q = a·guide + b` over every window, with `a = cov/(var + eps)`: where the guide varies a lot the output follows it, and where it barely varies the output is the local mean. It is the obvious fit for two of these stages - chroma guided by luma at two radii, and luma guided by itself - and no stage here is one.

**What it cannot do is why.** It decides "signal or noise" from a *local variance*, and it can only protect an edge it can see: a red wall meeting a grey roof is a large step in colour and a small one in luma, so a 65-pixel window spanning both fits one model across the pair and pours red onto the roof. Bounding that with an amplitude cap - the coarse pass moving a colour by no more than 2% of full scale - is bounded rather than fixed. On real frames what survives the cap is a visible desaturation and a green fringe along every high-contrast edge, both at the defaults it was tuned to, and both of them the same failure: a filter reasoning about colour a demosaic has already smeared across neighbouring pixels.

**One finding from it is worth keeping.** It was tried for the sharpener too, and failed instructively: an edge-aware base layer for an unsharp mask is halo-free *because* it keeps the edge in the base, so what is left in `I − guided(I)` is texture and noise and no edge at all - sharpening everything except the thing that needed sharpening. The unit tests caught it before a picture did. That is the argument the deconvolution below rests on.

#### Deconvolution, not an unsharp mask

An unsharp mask has no model of what softened the picture. It adds back a scaled copy of the high frequencies, and the overshoot that leaves either side of an edge **is** the halo. Richardson-Lucy has a model - the point spread from the capture, demosaic and any resample - and iterates towards the image that, blurred by it, would have produced the one in hand. The edge comes back *steeper* rather than merely higher-contrast. It is what RawTherapee calls capture sharpening.

What it needs to be usable here:

- **Denoising first.** Not a preference - RL will invert grain as readily as blur, so anything left in luma at this point is what gets sharpened.
- **Anti-ringing that bends rather than cuts.** RL's maximum-likelihood solution rings against a hard edge. Each output is held near the range its own neighbourhood already spanned, through a knee (`image::SHARPEN_OVERSHOOT`) rather than a clamp: a hard clamp pins every textured pixel the blend pushes out of range to its window's extreme, and skin comes back as flat patches with hard borders.
- **The observation is never smoothed.** Richardson-Lucy still produces its plain estimate, with no damped update or band filtering. What gets admitted is only the detail added back from that estimate. `base::sharpen_noise` carries the frame's fitted shot and read variance through its white balance, camera matrix and PQ anchor. `sharpen.slang` reads each RGB channel through the existing 16-bit inverse-PQ table, so a saturated shadow is measured at its three local curve slopes rather than as one neutral code. The anti-ringing pass already finds each pixel's original 5×5 range; only the share of that range above six measured pixel sigmas is admitted. The blend is `observed + support·amount·(estimate − observed)`, so declining unsupported recovery returns the samples exactly as they arrived rather than replacing them with a smoother answer.

`sharpening` blends the supported result through `image::SHARPEN_GAIN`: the middle of the track is the supported deconvolution as computed, and the top extrapolates to twice its difference from the frame. The range is measured after the resize and lens gather, on the raster being sharpened. Variance follows the samples that made that raster: a reduced Bayer pixel averages one red, two greens and one blue; an X-Trans block always averages five greens but may hold only one of either chroma at some phases, which is the conservative count used; a later area resize reduces each again by its footprint. A `full` rendition therefore sees the noise its reduction leaves and `max` sees the native-resolution model. The range is already inside the deconvolution's existing 42-pixel halo, so a loupe window and the whole frame make the same decision.

The bound is held against the ISO 40000 Bayer fixture under auto denoise and `Blacks = -27`, at native resolution, on the dark crop at `(1200, 5150)`: Sharpening 50 may raise the 99th-percentile horizontal green-channel code Laplacian by at most 1.1× over Sharpening 0. The supported render reads 1.00× on the 3080. Clean axial and diagonal edges and a point are separately required to retain substantial recovery under a nonzero noise model, so support cannot satisfy the noise bound by declining every correction.

**The iteration count is most of the cost.** Ten iterations of four dispatches each ship, which is 400ms of a 24MP render, and `image::Strengths::halo` - what a loupe tile grows its decode window by - is linear in the count. Fewer iterations under a larger gain is not the same picture: support is applied once to the finished difference and cannot turn an unconverged estimate into the one those iterations would have produced.

Richardson-Lucy still earns its four convolutions per iteration: at a matched 1.4x grain it recovers 0.835 where the best five-tap regularised inverse recovers 0.476, which is what says a one-pass sharpener is not the cheaper answer.

**The step's exponent is where the cheap convergence is.** RL's update is a ratio and its exponent compounds, so raising the correction to a power takes the same step more than once: squaring the correction recovers at N iterations what the plain step recovers at 2N, and amplifies a noise field by the same factor to three decimals - two iterations for a multiply rather than for four more convolutions. It is written into `sharpen_correct` as a square rather than a `pow`, which slangc emits as a call the driver lowers to an exp2/log2 pair; two is therefore the only exponent implemented. A cube does not converge: on a blurred step edge it reads 0.494 of the step at ten iterations, 1.000 at twenty and 0.494 again at eighty, which is an estimate oscillating rather than settling.

**The blur it inverts is measured, and where it is measured is the whole of whether the number means anything.** `base::measure_edge_spread` votes the 10-90 distance of every clean, monotone, near-vertical rise in the frame into a histogram and takes the sharpest decile: a scene edge can be softer than the point spread and never harder, so the sharp tail is the optics and the soft majority is the subject. In linear light, where an integrated Gaussian's 10-90 is 2.563 sigma exactly, and on the demosaiced frame, because what a deconvolution running after the demosaic actually meets is both. Once per photograph, in `open::measure`, so a rendition and the loupe that predicts it deconvolve the same blur.

**Not off the mosaic, which is the tempting place and the wrong one.** Two diagonal green neighbours bound sigma by their ratio - at most `exp(d^2 / 2.sigma^2)` - and that reads as a free estimate taken before any interpolation has smeared anything. It is not one. The bound is reached by a *point source* and by nothing else, so what such a ratio reports is how point-like the sharpest thing in the frame happens to be; and the green lattice is two pixels apart, where the rise being measured is under two pixels wide. Measured that way over four frames: 1.26, nothing at all, 1.21 and 0.81, where an independently written edge probe reads 0.59, 0.96, 1.07 and 0.68 - out by 2.1x on the sharpest of the four, and uncorrelated with sharpness rather than merely biased. The edge spread reads 0.67, 0.94, 1.62 and 0.65 against those same four: three within 13%, and the fourth clamped away by the five-tap ceiling either way.

Above 1 the blend is an extrapolation past the estimate, which is what makes the anti-ringing knee above load-bearing rather than a formality: it is applied to the blend and not to the estimate, so the extra reach cannot leave the neighbourhood's own range whatever the slider says. The knee bounds amplitude; measured support decides whether reconstructed detail is admitted. Neither changes the observation underneath it.

#### The noise is measured, not predicted

`image::sigma_from` takes a high-pass residual and reads its **median** absolute value. The median is what makes it work on a photograph: edges and texture are a minority of pixels and arbitrarily large, so they drag a mean anywhere, while the median sits in the flat majority where the only signal is noise. It is the estimator wavelet shrinkage has used for thirty years, and it costs one box mean and a histogram.

It was the denoise's regularisation; what still asks is the **defringe**, which needs each channel's noise separately to debias its regression - green's noise enters the regressor and the response with opposite signs where red's enters only one, and a single luma figure cannot express that. The denoise fits its own model now, on the mosaic, against a physical noise process rather than against a residual (§10.9.1).

**It replaced an ISO scaling, and is strictly better.** The ISO was there to solve a real problem - one number for a five-stop library is far too heavy at one end or does nothing at the other - but by the time this runs, the frame has been demosaiced, resampled (which averages some of the noise away) and graded, possibly by several stops. None of that is in the ISO. Asking the pixels needs no reference ISO, no cap, and no special case for a body that records nothing.

#### What the guided filters were worth, per plane

**The baseline §10.9.1 has to beat, and the shape of the table is the argument.** The metric is high-frequency energy - mean squared difference against a σ2 blur of the same crop - taken **separately on luma and chroma**, over two flat regions and one detailed one, on a 24MP EOS R10 frame shot at dusk at ISO 2000. Per-plane matters: an RGB measurement is dominated by luma, which is how an earlier version of this section certified a chroma denoise that had changed nothing visible.

| | flat sky | flat water | detailed roofline |
|---|---|---|---|
| **chroma** | −100% | −100% | −97% |
| **luma** | −99.5% | −25% | +19% |

That is the shape the whole section is trying to reach: noise gone from the flat regions in both planes, and the detailed one *up* despite the denoise having run over it. Decomposed on the roofline, luma HF goes 144 → 121 under the denoise alone and up to 171 once the sharpen runs - past where it started, and made of detail rather than grain.

**The flat-water column was the tuning, and deliberately not the best number available.** At twice the shrinkage it read −53% instead of −25%, and a dark roof at 100% had visibly lost its shingle texture. Grain reads as a photograph; smearing reads as a fault. The metric cannot see that difference, which is why the constant was set by looking and the number recorded as the cost of doing so - and it is the same reason the Detail sliders default where they do rather than higher.

**This is the ceiling of the approach, and it is worth saying so.** Every artefact chased out of this chain was a local filter deciding "signal or noise" from a local statistic, and being wrong in a spatially varying way: patchy smoothing from an unstable variance, colour bleed from a guide that could not see an iso-luminant edge, ringing from a deconvolution with no noise model. Each has a guard now, and each guard is a constant that was tuned by looking. What remains on a dark roof at 100% is fine grain this class of filter cannot separate from the roof's own texture, because at that scale and amplitude there is nothing local to separate them by - and the settings are deliberately short of the point where it tries to, since the failure on that side is smearing and the failure on this side is grain.

#### What it costs

| | wall |
|---|---|
| neither | 605ms |
| both | 1294ms |

**It runs in horizontal strips, and that is what makes it affordable at all.** Every stage works on `f32` planes and the guided filter needs several live at once, so on whole frames the scratch is a dozen planes of whatever it is handed: 39MB each at a 3840px rendition, 96MB each at 24MP native. Measured, that was 671MB and **1576MB** peak RSS, against a §10.7 budget of 954MB for `max` - and a 61MP body would have been 2.5x worse again.

Every stage here is *local with bounded reach*, so a strip that carries enough context produces exactly what a whole-frame pass would. `strip_interior` picks a row count from a 64MB scratch budget - solved for `interior + 2 * halo`, not for the interior alone, since the halo is fixed in *rows* and budgeting only the interior lets the peak grow linearly in the frame's **width**, measured at 2.7x the budget on a 9504-wide frame. So the peak stops scaling with the frame in either direction:

| | neither | both |
|---|---|---|
| `full` at 3840 | 339MB | **339MB** |
| `max` at native resolution | 540MB | **573MB** |

At the rendition size it costs nothing measurable, and at native resolution 33MB rather than a gigabyte. At the widest chroma radius the schema allowed, native resolution measured **563MB** - the same, where before the budget was solved properly it gained another ~205MB. The duplicated work at the seams - each strip also computes its halo and throws it away - is **~3%** of wall clock.

The figures in this subsection are an upper bound, not a measurement of the current chain: its halo is the deconvolution's alone, so the strips are far taller and what they hold is far smaller than these numbers describe.

One case the budget cannot honour: a very wide frame at a very high strength floors at `3 * halo` rows of planes, because a strip may never be thinner than its own halo. That is enforced in `finish_in_strips` rather than only where the caller picks the height, since a strip below the halo would silently take its context from rows an earlier strip had already written over - measured at an interior of 10, rows wrong by 257 counts with no panic and no seam loud enough for the equivalence test to notice.

**The halo is the whole correctness argument, and it is bigger than it looks.** A guided filter of radius r reaches *2r*, not r: it box-means the input and then box-means the fit. The chroma path composes three of them - guided by a luma that was itself filtered, then filtered again at the coarse radius - so the reaches add, giving `2 * (6 + 4 + 32) = 84` rows at the default. Richardson-Lucy adds its point spread twice per iteration, 40 rows over ten, plus the anti-ringing window.

Two things follow that are not obvious. The **noise estimate has to stay global** - it is accumulated into one histogram strip by strip, because a per-strip estimate would have each strip denoise by a different amount and seam. And the rows a strip writes are the *next* strip's context, so the originals of the last `halo` of them are kept back before the write; without that, every strip after the first reads its neighbour's output as if it were the input.

**Two tests, because the obvious one is not enough.** `strips_produce_what_a_whole_frame_would_have` drives one frame both ways and requires agreement within a single count - not bit-for-bit, because a plane of a different height splits into different bands and a running sum accumulates in a different order, which moves the last bit of an f32.

That test is a seam check and **not** the halo's guard, which is worth stating because it reads like one and is not: measured under mutation, it still passes with the halo cut to a sixth of what it should be. The coarse chroma pass supplies 64 of the 84 rows and is capped at 2% of full scale, so however wrong its context is, the output barely moves. `the_halo_covers_how_far_the_filters_actually_reach` is the real guard: it moves a single pixel at `halo + 1` rows and requires the top row not to change, which measures the *dependency* rather than the difference it happens to make. That one fails as soon as the halo is short by a few rows, and it also pins the direction that matters - influence attenuates long before it ends, so a pixel already inside the deconvolution's reach can fail to shift the top row by a whole count while the dependency is still there.

**~690ms for the pair, roughly doubling the job**, and the honest reading is that this is expensive - though it is time rather than memory, which the strips took care of. Two earlier versions were cheaper and worse: LibRaw's wavelet denoise cost ~400ms on its own and was invisible or waxy with nothing in between, and a chroma-only Gaussian with an unsharp mask cost ~200ms and left the luma grain that is most of what the eye objects to. The settings exist so a library that would rather have the throughput can say so.

### 10.9.1 The denoise, on the mosaic

**Sensor noise is per-photosite, and a demosaic is the last moment that is true.** Interpolation averages neighbouring sites to invent the two colours each one did not record, which correlates the noise across pixels and turns a chroma error into a coloured smudge with real spatial extent. Anything §10.9 does about noise is downstream of that, reasoning about colour already smeared - which is why a guided filter there needs an amplitude cap to stop a red wall reaching a grey roof, and why a green fringe survives the cap on ordinary frames.

GALOSH runs on the mosaic rawler hands back - after `condition` has taken out the black level, applied the as-shot balance and normalised to the unit interval, and before the demosaic - which is the only window in which the mosaic exists (`galosh::denoise`), as the Slang compute kernels in `slang/galosh/` driven by `galosh.rs`.

**Blind, so there is no profile to ship.** Phase 0 fits the sensor's Poisson-Gaussian model off the frame itself: the shot-noise slope from how per-block variance rises with per-block level, and the read-noise intercept from the Laplacians of the pixels the frame's own tenth percentile calls dark. darktable's equivalent is a per-camera database, which is GPL and could not be used here anyway; fitting per frame also answers the harder question, since two exposures from one body do not have the same noise.

#### `pass12` keeps its block in registers, and that is the difference between shipping and not

`pass12` is the shrinkage, and it is where the frame's time goes: sixteen cycle-spin phases, two passes, a 64-point transform per 8x8 block. With the block in `float block[64]` and its median in a second 63-element array it ran a 12MP frame in **10.3s**, and a 24MP one lost the device to its watchdog.

The cause is not the arithmetic. A GPU gives a **dynamically-indexed local array a scratch allocation in device memory** - then takes the rest of the kernel's working set with it, because they spill together. Every one of the ~2600 element accesses a block costs becomes an uncoalesced round trip. So every index is a constant: the block is sixteen `vec4f` registers, and the median is a sorting network over them rather than a partial sort of an array.

| | 12.6MP | 24MP |
|---|---|---|
| local arrays | 10.3s | device lost |
| vector registers | **1.29s** | **2.38s** |

Two smaller choices are for memory or latency rather than for speed. `k16_inverse_fused` folds the final chroma upsample into the inverse, which avoids materialising three *full-resolution* planes - 720MB at 61MP, every value of which is read once at the coordinate it was written. And `irls_seed` runs on the device rather than reading back mid-frame, so a frame is one command buffer and one readback rather than three submissions with a pipeline stall between them.

**Two of the sixteen phases run, and they run down the diagonal.** The sixteen are estimates of the same block and averaging two lands where averaging all sixteen does, judged end to end against the camera's own JPEG. What the averaging is *for*, though, is that no block boundary falls in the same place twice, and `pass12`'s phase index is a quotient and a remainder off one counter - so a pair strided the cheap way differs in the column alone, every row boundary lands on the same row of the frame, and the residue is constant along a row. That is invisible to a frame mean and unmistakable once the sharpen's amount multiplies it: a shadow reads as horizontal lines. `PHASE_DIAGONAL` puts the second phase at (2, 2) instead. Measured as the mean second difference of a crop's row means against its column means, a ratio of one being no direction at all: column-only reads 2.46, the diagonal pair 0.79, four phases 0.92 - and the pair costs ~45ms less than four on a 24MP mosaic. **The lesson is about the metric, not the constant**: the comparison that chose two phases was a mean over a frame, and a one-count pattern that follows the rows moves a mean by nothing while being the first thing an eye finds.

**Colour at zero skips the chroma regression outright.** `smoothstep_blend_3p` at `slider <= 0` takes its first anchor, and its green-difference reconstruction then returns that anchor exactly - so the half-resolution LOESS, which is a fifth of the denoise at 89ms of 396 on a 24MP mosaic, computes a plane the blend overwrites. Skipped, with `c_h` bound straight into the inverse instead: ~64ms, bit-identical, and `at_zero_colour_the_blend_returns_the_chroma_it_was_handed` is what holds the identity the skip rests on.

#### The strength belongs to the photograph

A frame at 12800 and one at base ISO want different answers, so a library setting could only ever be right for one of them. `EditDoc` carries **`luminanceNoise` and `colourNoise`**, 0 to 100 and nullable, and **`sharpening`** sits beside them on the same argument, since how much blur there is to invert is the photograph's business too.

**The luminance scale's landmark sits above its middle.** The plane is normalised to its fitted sigma before the shrinkage runs, so a position on the track is the fraction of the frame's own measured noise to remove, and the calibrated point where exactly that much is treated as noise is **62.5** - `TRACK_TOP` being 1.6, short of twice calibrated, because the track ran past what a photograph survives. That much is ISO-adaptive by construction, the same relative shrinkage at base ISO as on a pushed frame.

**The top half is headroom against the fit being wrong, not a suggestion.** An envelope over the quietest blocks is a good estimate and not an infallible one: a frame whose quietest tenth still holds texture reads low, and stopping the track where the estimate says it should would leave that frame under-denoised with nothing to be done about it. Twice the calibrated point is reachable, and a reader who goes there is overriding a measurement rather than turning a knob past a limit.

**There is no default, because no fixed pair can be one.** The shrinkage removes a *ratio*, so what a reader sees at a position is that ratio times however much noise there was: measured on the first band of an à trous decomposition of an undenoised render, base-ISO daylight sits at 0.5 to 1.0 and these night frames at 4.5 to 6.9, so one number is either nothing on the first or a third of the job on the second. On the colour half it is not even a matter of degree - the amount is a *distance* up a chroma pyramid rather than a ratio of anything, so a setting strong enough to lift the mottle off a night sky smooths real colour out of a clean frame whatever its noise.

So the document stores null and the decode resolves it: `EditDoc`'s `luminanceNoise` and `colourNoise` are nullable and default to null, `galosh::Detail` carries that null down through every entry point, and the decode answers it with `NoiseModel::suggested_amounts` off the fit it has in hand - measured ahead of the filtering rather than on its way past, since an unset slider is not a number until the frame has been measured. A frame clean enough is declined on both halves and filtered not at all. What the decode resolved comes back on `edit::PreparedHeader`, which is what the Detail panel shows and what its reset arrow goes back to: null rather than a number, so the answer keeps following the photograph.

Where a reader does move one, the failures are not symmetric in either direction. Short of the mark a frame keeps grain and past it the frame smears, and only the first still reads as a photograph; and between the two sliders, grain in luma has that defence where colour mottle has none, so the colour half runs ahead of the one whose over-reach costs texture - by `galosh::COLOUR_LEAD`, which is what sets the two apart on any given frame.

**That only works because sigma is right, and getting it right was the harder half.** The estimator this shipped with took one median of the three-tap Laplacian over the whole frame, and on a detailed photograph the median pixel is not a quiet one: measured against a per-block envelope over a 32-frame library it reads **2.6 to 4.6 times high**. An over-estimate there is not a mild over-smoothing, because a block whose own deviation falls to the assumed noise has its whole AC zeroed rather than shrunk - so an inflated sigma flattens every block quieter than the texture that fooled it. Both paths take the envelope now: each 8x8 block's own median Laplacian, then the quiet tail rather than the middle, since every block carries noise and only some carry a picture as well. On the editor's side that envelope is taken per level and where the frame is built, for the reasons below.

**The ramp is a gate and a slope, and it is not "more ISO, more denoise"** - that rule picks 95 on an ISO 25600 frame, well past where its texture survives. `NoiseModel::suggested_amount` reads the *read-noise floor* rather than the shot-noise slope, because only one of the two is measured robustly: the slope comes from a regression of per-block variance against per-block level and collapses on a frame that is mostly black, which is exactly the photograph a denoise is for. Below `GATE` the frame is declined outright rather than put through the chain to be left alone, which is where this library's base-ISO frames sit; above it the position rises linearly across `SPAN`, fitted so the noisiest frame measured lands around 40 on the luminance track. That errs towards grain deliberately: what is lost to under-denoising luminance still reads as a photograph, and what is lost to over-denoising it is the texture itself.

Separate, because the two answer to different complaints. Grain in luma reads as a photograph and is worth keeping some of; colour mottle has no such defence and wants all the smoothing it can be given. In the kernels they are genuinely different knobs rather than one scaled twice: the luma number is a shrinkage threshold in units of the frame's own fitted noise, and the colour number is a walk along four anchors - noisy, a half-resolution regression, a quarter-resolution level and an eighth - so its unit is *how far colour may be smoothed, in scales*.

**The top third of that walk is the only thing that reaches mottle.** Every level below the eighth regresses over tens of sensor pixels at most, so chroma structure wider than that survives whatever the slider says: measured on `DSC05282`, an ISO 4000 night frame, the à trous band whose hole is 32 comes back at 0.35 codes of 255 where the three-anchor walk ends, against 0.13 at the end of the four. A reader looking at a night sky sees that difference, and no other stage removes it - the defringe is an edge. The two coarse levels are built only where the walk reaches them, each being two downsamples, a whole LOESS and a guided upsample back.

**The very top of the track is not free, and the ramp stops short of it.** The eighth level comes up two guided upsamples, each carrying the half-pixel siting bias `k16_jbu_3p` records, so its chroma arrives translated against luma by more than one level's worth. Over dense coloured detail that reads as a hue shift rather than as smoothing - on the same frame's planting the greens go olive, and the chroma roughness at the pixel's own scale doubles between sliders 75 and 85, where the smoothstep first hands the eighth level the majority of the blend. Correcting the siting wants a wider kernel renormalised for quarter phases, not an edit to the table.

**A coarse level may move the chroma by about the noise, and no further.** Its planes are box downsamples, which average across any colour step that does not fall on their grid, and the guided upsample only clamps to those mixed samples - so taken whole, the eighth spread a saturated green on grey across sixteen pixels, which is haze on every coloured edge. The blend therefore takes each level's move against the level below through `exp(-|d|^2 / 2 reach^2)` (`LEVEL_REACH`, in the GAT's unit noise): mottle is a move of a sigma or so and mostly passes (a night sky keeps about half of what the ungated walk removed at 32 pixels, its low-frequency chroma standing further off the noise model than that), a real step is a move of tens and is refused, and whatever a straddling sample mixed can shift a pixel by at most ~0.6 reach. `a_colour_step_does_not_bleed_off_the_coarse_levels` pins it.

**A site's chroma carries its luma detail, so the walk is bracketed.** The three site differences are row, column and diagonal differences of the four photosites, so a brightness change across a site - a hair strand darkening one column of it - lands in them as surely as a hue does. A pyramid smoothing them as hue takes that detail with the noise, and the inverse rebuilds each site flat: skin and hair come back painted, with the site grid showing through. `chroma_detail` predicts that part from the denoised stride-1 luma before the walk and puts back exactly what it took after the blend, so only the colour is smoothed. Each photosite is modelled as its colour's local level times one shared modulation, since on a coloured surface a line moves the dominant channel and barely the rest, and the site's own one-pixel difference is read off the two-pixel luma box with a seven-tap derivative matched to `i tan(pi f)` - a plain central difference reaches `cos^2(pi f)` of it. Detail past the box's null, towards 0.5 cycles, is in the site differences alone and no prediction reaches it. Bayer only; the prediction costs three half-resolution planes. `texture_on_a_coloured_surface_survives_the_colour_denoise` pins it: 0.69 of a texture kept against 0.33 unbracketed.

**X-Trans and Foveon get no denoise at all.** Every phase here pairs rows and columns into 2x2 CFA sites - the transform, the chroma extraction, the per-slot dark reference - and a 6x6 array is not that. Declining is the honest answer; a filter that assumed the wrong periodicity would produce a maze pattern rather than a denoise. The same is true of an adapter that cannot offer the 25.6KB of workgroup storage `pass12` needs, which is asked about rather than assumed, since a validation failure there would be fatal.

#### The editor runs this denoiser, not one of its own

**There is no second denoise.** With the mosaic not crossing to the client a Detail slider cannot re-run *this*, and the editor would have to filter the prepared frame with an sRGB front-end instead - one sigma split by a fixed ratio where this fits shot and read noise separately, two chroma channels where this has three transform terms, no per-slot dark reference because a demosaiced frame has no slots. That preview is an honest approximation of the export and the two are not expected to agree.

They are one now. The RAW is decoded in the tab, so the mosaic *is* on the client: the open denoises it here, at the reader's own Detail, through the call `job::Base::build` makes. A slider re-runs the decode from the mosaic down rather than filtering a frame that has already been demosaiced, coded and warped, and it arrives a band at a time - each strip bit-for-bit the pixels the whole frame would have had, which `a_band_of_a_held_mosaic_is_the_frame_it_was_cut_from` pins.

**What that cost, and why it was still right.** A re-prepare is 3587ms on a 24MP frame against a chain of eight dispatches that was interactive. The preview being an approximation was not a stable position: every question about whether the editor agreed with the export had to be answered by argument, and the two mappings from slider to kernel units needed a shared fixture to hold them together. One denoise has no such question.

**Which the loupe was not, until the tile stopped fitting its own noise.** A tile takes this same path with the demosaic and the denoise restricted to the crop - that restriction is the whole reason a magnified region costs about 110ms rather than three seconds - but Phase 0 is a whole-*region* reduction, so a 512px crop was fitting shot and read noise off a few hundred thousand photosites of one part of one picture. Measured over the fixtures, a tile's own fit came out between **0.49 and 1.51** times the frame's, and that ratio is the strength it is then denoised at. So the magnifier disagreed with the export it exists to predict, and the disagreement moved as the reader panned, each pan being a new region and a new fit. No halo fixes it; the reduction is not local.

What fixes it is the frame's fit. A whole-frame decode measures it (`galosh::Fit::Only`, the same chain stopped after Phase 2, so it is a prefix of the denoise rather than a second estimator that agrees today) and it travels two ways: on the prepared header as seven numbers, which the editor hands straight back on each tile request, and in the photograph's own analysis file, which is where a caller with no open behind it - a rendition's loupe, over HTTP - reads it (§10.10).

**A tile is a rectangle of the photograph, and every stage that reasoned about "the frame" was reasoning about the crop.** Each of these was its own fault, and the pattern is the same every time: a stage reduces or scales itself against the buffer it is handed, and for a loupe tile that buffer is the few hundred thousand pixels the reader is pointing at. Measured against the rendition itself - `a_tile_is_graded_as_the_rendition_is`, which asserts equality rather than closeness - on a 512px crop of the fixtures, as the mean difference in counts of 65535:

| what the crop answered for itself | was | what it takes to answer properly |
|---|---|---|
| the levels - the white it is coded against, the peak it rolls into | **9333** | the editor measures them at the open and sends them per tile |
| `peak.slang`'s scene peak, which the roll-off compresses into the display | **1343** | the tick measures it every draw; the loupe reads it back and sends it |
| the sharpen, which the tile skipped entirely | **213** | run it, on a window grown by its own 42-pixel reach |
| the lens warp, applied as though the crop were the photograph | **153** | the frame's gather over this window, from the region it reads |
| `detail.slang`'s working texture and the grid it partitions on | **27** | a step the photograph decides, and a window aligned to it |

Three of them are numbers that cross the API as query parameters, and the other two are the shape of the tile itself.

**The sharpen and the presence sliders decide how much is decoded.** A deconvolution reads past what it writes, by `image::Strengths::halo` (twice the kernel radius per iteration, plus the clamp's own stencil) and the guided filter reaches two box means and a blur - eighteen texels, which at a 512-long working texture is about 216 pixels of a 6000-pixel photograph. A tile cut at exactly the rectangle asked for rings along every edge and fits its Clarity against an edge the picture does not have, and the loupe's tile is only half again its glass, so at high magnification both are inside what the reader is looking at. `job::grown` therefore asks for the window plus whatever the stages after the gather reach, and `job::graded` cuts it back to the rectangle asked for *after the grade* - so the blur is built from a frame that still has its surroundings. The presence reach is paid only when one of the three is off zero, where it is four times the area of a 400px tile.

**The lens is where a tile stops being a crop of the sensor.** What the client names is a rectangle of the *corrected* picture - its frame is `edit::open`'s, with the warp already materialised - so the gather has to be the photograph's own, restricted to that window (`base::Gather::window`), and what to decode is then whatever that window reads (`image::lens_footprint`) rather than the window itself. Correcting a crop as though it were the frame bends it by the whole correction across a few hundred pixels and lifts its corners as if they were the photograph's; measured on the Canon fixture it left the tile registered a couple of pixels from its own render, and dropping the correction entirely measured the same, because the error was never the warp but where it was applied from.

A window and a whole frame are one kernel with three numbers between them, which is what makes "the photograph's own gather" a fact rather than a claim - and `a_window_of_the_gather_is_the_frame_it_was_cut_from` asserts it bit for bit. The one thing that has to be spelled carefully is where the arithmetic happens: the tap position is computed in the *photograph's* coordinates and only then moved into the buffer being read. Folded the other way - the window's centre carrying its own origin - the two are equal in exact arithmetic and not in `f32`, and the gather truncates into `u16`, so one ulp either side of an integer is a whole count. Measured on the 6000px Canon: 0.68% of a 512px tile's samples a count from the render it predicts, worth up to 76 counts where the grade had a shadow to expand. The CPU gather had the same shape and never showed it, `f64` being wide enough to hide it.

**And the blur's grid has to be the photograph's.** Two things there, and the second is the one that took the measuring: `detail.slang` sizes its window as a fraction of the working texture's long edge, which for a tile is a twelfth of the picture the export filters - fixed by carrying the photograph's own long edge in the uniform (`edit.detail_long`). What that alone does not fix is *which pixels* land in each texel: the partition was the frame divided by the texture, so a window's texel boundaries fell between the photograph's and every texel averaged a different set of pixels. It is a step now (`edit.detail_step`, `gpu::detail_step`), which is translation-invariant, and `job::grown` snaps the window's origin to a whole number of them. The same rounding bit the *read* as well - `textureSampleLevel` scales uv by the texture's own size, and that size is the frame rounded up to a whole texel - so the grade samples the blur at the texel the partition puts a pixel in rather than at its uv. With the scale right and the grid aligned the residual went 27, then 6, then zero.

**The levels travel the same way, and they were the louder half of the same mistake.** The levels are a quantile of whatever frame `job::Base::build` is holding, and for a tile that frame is the crop: over a dark part of the Sony fixture it reads a diffuse white of 2605 against the photograph's 8133, and a scene peak of 3013 against 13783. Everything downstream divides by the first and rolls into the second, so the loupe lifted a shadow to reference white with no roll-off left to apply - a glass full of clipped white over a picture that was almost black. Measured as the coded base, a tile handed the frame's levels is bit-identical to the frame's own pixels there, where one measuring its own sits 7187 counts of 65535 away on average. So a tile request carries them beside the fit (`levels=white,peak`), and both are refused - `tone::Levels::usable`, `galosh::NoiseFit::usable` - rather than trusted, since they cross the API from a client and a white of zero would divide the frame by it.

**A cropped render is a very large tile, and takes the same route.** Everything below the decode costs what it is given, so a photograph cropped to a quarter was denoising, coding, defringing and gathering four times what it shows. `job::Base::cropped` asks for the rectangle the geometry reads (`image::geometry_footprint`, the output's border swept through the mapping - which bounds the interior, the crop and the straighten being affine and the keystone projective) and hands it to `crate::tile`. The grade then applies the geometry over a *window*: `geometry.slang` takes the photograph's own dimensions for the crop fractions and subtracts the window's origin from the position it computes, in that order, for the reason `warp.slang` splits its centre from its origin.

It declines rather than approximating, and three ways. Nothing cropped, which is most photographs. A target wanting the picture smaller than the sensor gives it, where the window's origin would have to be scaled by a non-integer ratio and the two routes would part by a sub-pixel shift - worth having later, behind a measurement. And a photograph nothing has measured yet, since a window can read none of the levels, the noise, the aberration or the match: no user path reaches a cropped render cold, a crop being made in the editor whose open measures all four, and if one ever does the whole-frame decode stores them and the next render takes this route.

`a_render_that_decoded_only_its_crop_is_the_one_that_decoded_everything` asserts the two bit for bit, and asserts it **twice** - the frame handed to the grade, over the rectangle the geometry reads, and then the graded picture. That split is not tidiness: a single end-to-end number cannot tell a decode fault from a grade fault, and this was 296 counts for long enough to be chased through both. What it turned out to be was neither. The frames were identical; the *test* was comparing a freshly fitted camera match against the same match through the analysis file, whose chroma lattice is kept at the `f16` its texture holds. Two real faults did fall out on the way: the missing `SHRINK_LATTICE` rounding on region decodes (§10.9.1), and a window taking the presence blur's scale from its own long edge rather than the photograph's.

Nothing measures the prepared frame's noise: by the time a frame is prepared, the noise has been removed on the mosaic. `gpu_fixture.rs` pins the grade rather than the denoise, which is the claim it is for.

#### The reader chooses which filter those positions drive

The Detail panel names a denoiser, and the document carries it (`galosh::Denoiser`, defaulting to GALOSH, so a library edited before the choice existed renders as it did). Both filters run in the same window - the conditioned mosaic, before the demosaic - and both are driven by the same fit, so the choice reaches only as far as the filtering itself (`decode_rawler::denoise_over`).

**PMRID is a published network rather than a model of this sensor** (`pmrid.rs`, `slang/pmrid.slang`, weights by `bun run get:pmrid`). A rendition's binary carries the weights, because a machine missing a file beside it would render a different photograph rather than fail; the editor fetches them instead, from a hashed name of their own beside the module (`scripts/hash-pkg.ts`), so the four megabytes are cached apart from a module whose name changes with every line of Rust and are never fetched at all by a reader who stays on GALOSH. Ninety-two layers over four planes: the 2x2 CFA site split into red, both greens and blue at half the frame's size, and a residual predicted over them. It is **7x faster than GALOSH over a 61MP frame** - 569ms against 3920 - which is why it is offered for every rendition rather than only where a reader is looking.

What the two positions mean is not the same on both. GALOSH reads Luminance as a multiple of the noise it measured, calibrated so 62.5 is once; PMRID predicted a residual whose size it already decided, so a position is the share of it to keep and 100 is the whole. `reap` splits that residual before mixing: what the four photosites of a site agree on is its reading of the light, what they disagree on is its reading of the colour, and the second survives every strength where the first is what melts a twig.

**Two thirds of it is matrix multiplication, and a rendition puts that on the tensor cores where the card has them.** Forty-three of the ninety-two layers are 1x1 convolutions, which over an NCHW arena are already `C = A * B`; `slang/spirv/pmrid_coop.slang` says so with `OpCooperativeMatrixMulAddKHR`, which WGSL cannot spell, so it is SPIR-V handed to the driver as it stands - on the device wgpu already opened, so nothing crosses the bus for it (`gpu::asked_features`, `pmrid::coop_kernels`). A 25MP frame is 256ms that way against 285 on the WGSL pass. **The editor never takes that arm**: a browser offers neither the instruction nor passthrough shaders, so a page runs the WGSL pointwise pass, and `the_tensor_arm_denoises_what_the_wgsl_arm_does` pins the two to 0.04 of a code of 255 - the accumulator is `half` on one side and `float` on the other, and that is the whole of the difference.

**Three things about it are approximate, and are why it is a choice rather than the default.** The network was trained on one sensor's raw photosites where ours are black-subtracted, normalised and balanced, so the gains are divided back out before the pack and multiplied in after. Its input is variance-stabilised to its own calibration's ISO 1600 anchor, a polynomial in *that* sensor's ISO; what stands in is this frame's own fitted model converted into the same `k`/`sigma` pair, off green. And a tile is grown by 128 mosaic pixels on every side because four halvings reach that far, which costs 1.56x the area and is what stops a seam.

### 10.9.2 Repairs (`repair.rs`, `repair_solve.rs`)

A repair is a spot or a thing the reader drew a loop around, filled from elsewhere in the same photograph. The document holds each one whole: the loop drawn, the seam the fill lands inside, where the fill is read from, and a gain that brings its light to the light around it (`RepairSchema`). Nothing the search needed is kept - a loop is solved again if the reader asks - so a document holds only what a render reads.

**Positions are on a grid of the picture as the lens left it** (`px::Stored`): 65535 steps across the long edge, the short edge in proportion, before the crop, the straighten and the turn - for a composite, its canvas. Every frame the chain writes is a picture of that at some size, so one repair lands in the same place on the editor's frame, on a level of a composite, on a rendition of any size and on a loupe's window, through `Point::onto` and nothing else. Integers, because the document is replicated and re-parsed on every write; a step is 0.15 of a photosite across a 60MP frame.

**Applied after the sharpen, on both hosts, by one shader** (`repair.slang`). That is the one place a window and a whole frame both reach: a window leaves `base::prepare` sharpened (`tile::prepared_on_device`), and a whole rendition - a composite's included - is sharpened per target (`hdr::Cut::from_base`, then `job::repaired`). A repair's fill is mixed in light rather than in code, with a feather centred on the seam by the merge's own rule (`seam_weight.slang`), half at the seam and all of it a feather inside. The picture's border is not a seam: a seam reaching a cell at the edge is taken onto it (`repair_solve`), and the fill does not fade toward it, so a removal drawn past the edge is filled right up to it. The solve offers two cells, never over 1.5% of the long edge, which is as far as the reader's Blend slider goes, and the slider sets it as a share of the long edge. The fill lands a feather past the seam, which is how far a window has to hold its source. And every fill is read from a copy of the frame taken before any repair ran - so two repairs never read each other's work, and their order decides only which is on top.

**A window grows to hold its fills** (`repair::reaching`). A seam reaching a loupe's tile or a band of a re-prepare reads its fill from wherever the solve found it, which can be across the photograph, so the window is widened to that place and the chain's own margin around it. `a_window_is_repaired_as_the_whole_frame_is` holds a window to the whole frame's pixels.

**A picture prepared on the server arrives without its repairs**, and the tab draws them, as it draws the geometry: `picture::prepared` hands over the picture, and every frame `HeldRaw` assembles from the tiles it holds has the document's repairs drawn over it (`setRepairs`), the rectangle grown by `reaching` so the tiles a fill reads are fetched with the ones on screen. So choosing between fills on a composite is a pass over tiles already in the tab, not a prepare per choice.

**The solve runs in the tab, on the frame the editor is drawing.** `repair_cells` averages the frame around the loop into cells a 24th of the loop across - so a dust spot and a person are searched at the same grain relative to themselves - and the rest is host arithmetic over those few thousand cells, for `patch_search`'s reason. The merge's two halves do the work: `Patch::over` compares the ground *around* the hole, never the thing in it, against everywhere within three loops of it; then for each place it matched, a two-label `MinCut` grows the fill out from the hole to wherever the photograph and the fill, brought to the same light, agree - the merge's seam cut between the photograph and a copy of itself. Up to four are offered, cheapest seam first. A place is refused where the hole would read from itself or from under another repair's seam, because what the pass reads there is the thing that repair removed. Where the frame is tiles of a level, it is first grown to all the search reads (`repair_solve::searched`, `setSearched`), which `a_window_holding_the_search_is_offered_what_the_frame_is` holds to offering exactly what the whole frame would.

**The reader can move a fill on offer, or where it is read from, as a clone stamp's pair.** The stage draws the source dashed with a line to the fill, and a drag of either redraws it moved at every step, each solved again with its place given rather than searched for (`solveRepair`'s `donor`): the same light matching and the same cut, at that one place, taken whatever it reads. One solve runs at a time and the last place is always solved; the seam is grown from the loop the reader drew, moved with the fill, never from the last solve's seam. Moving the fill keeps where it reads from where it was, and the other places on offer go with the loop they were found for; once it is let go, the loop is searched around where it now is, and what that finds is offered beside it.

**The loop is drawn on the stage as the reader has it** - cropped, straightened, turned, zoomed and panned - and taken onto the picture through the geometry the module draws with (`pictureOfOutput`); the seams come back onto the stage the same way (`outputOfPicture`, `image::output_at`). The tool takes the one pointer a pan would; the wheel, a pinch and a middle-button drag still move the view.

**A local open shows a repair by redrawing the repairs over the frame it holds** (`redrawRepairs`, `RetouchedFrame::redraw`): every original is pasted back, which is the frame before any repair, and the new set is drawn over it. No band is prepared again, so moving a fill, choosing another or dragging the blend is milliseconds; only a Detail or dust change runs the RAW chain.

**What a repair drew over is kept beside the frame, and cannot go stale** (`retouched_frame.rs`). The editor's frame is a `RetouchedFrame`, which owns its buffer and is written only by drawing repairs over a frame or by taking a band's rows - and every write goes through `repair::apply`, which cuts each repair's footprint out of the frame before painting over it (`copy_rect.slang`). So a whole prepare, a band and a frame assembled from tiles all leave each repair's original holding the pixels now under it. A removal's thumbnail is the frame with its original put back, and reopening one searches that same picture, so the stage keeps showing the fill while the other fills are found. Each fill on offer is shown the same way: the fill on show taken off a copy around the loop, and that fill drawn over it by the one pass (`drawn_instead`). In memory for the life of an open only. Where two repairs overlap, an original holds what was there before both, so putting one back uncovers the other inside its footprint.

### 10.10 What is measured once and kept (`photo_analysis.rs`)

**Four measurements decide most of what a render costs, and none of them is a function of the edit.** The camera match is about half a second (§10.8.1), the mosaic noise fit a quarter of one (§10.9.1), and the levels a quantile over a million samples (§10.7.1); the scene peak is a whole-frame reduction through the colour transform. Every path that renders the photograph wants all four - a rendition job, a rebuild after an edit, the editor's open, and worst of all the loupe, which asks again every time the reader moves. Measured on a 24MP CR3, a 400px tile is 626ms measuring them and 112ms handed them.

They are kept in one file per photograph, `<dataPath>/analysis/<photoId>.bba`, **beside `renditions/` rather than inside it**. That directory is a cache the orphan sweep may take at any moment (§10.6); this is not, and losing one is paying for it again on every later render.

**Two sections, because they do not go stale the same way.** `FromRaw` - the match and the noise fit - is what the file alone decides, and can only change when the photograph does. `FromRender` - the levels and the scene peak - is read off a decode, so each carries the white quantile and reference white it was measured under and answers nothing to a caller asking about others. What neither is sensitive to is the *reader*: the denoise and the colour transform are this pipeline's own, and a Detail amount is the only input either takes. That last part was measured rather than assumed, since a denoise does change the pixels a quantile reads - across the fixtures at Detail 0, 40 and 100, the widest spread in diffuse white is **1.3%**, which is 0.019 stops, and between 40 and 100 it is **0.065%**. Almost all of the movement is between denoising and not denoising at all, and none of it is a re-anchoring. So a crop, an exposure or a Detail slider reuses all four.

The scene peak is the one with a caveat, and it is `peak.slang`'s own: the measurement is taken **after** the exposure, so what is stored is the peak with the photographer's gain at rest. A job carrying one measures its own, and a tick has always remeasured over the candidates it kept. What storing it buys is that every render of a photograph *at rest* rolls its highlights off the same knee, which is the argument `tone::QUANTILE_SAMPLES` already makes at the other end of the range.

**The format is framed section by section**, so a build that meets a section it has never heard of steps over it on its length and keeps everything around it. That is what lets this grow - the next thing worth measuring is a section and not a version bump - without an older build refitting a photograph it could have read most of. A blob it cannot read at all is "nothing stored", and everything is measured the slow way, which is what happened before the file existed.

Writing merges rather than replaces (`PhotoAnalysis::filled_from`): the parts are measured by different callers at different times, and a writer that stored only what it held would drop whatever it had not measured itself. The HTTP route takes the later write, and what a lost race costs is one re-measurement.
